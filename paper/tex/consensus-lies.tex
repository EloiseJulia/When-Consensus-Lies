\begin{abstract}
``$4/4$ models agree'' reads as reassurance. Same-prompt interfaces such as ChatHub~\cite{chathub2026} send
one prompt to several language models (GPT, Claude, Gemini, \dots) and return their answers together, inviting
the user to read the apparent agreement as added confidence (some go further and compute an explicit consensus
or vote); collaborative and decision-support systems increasingly aggregate AI judgments the same way.
The reassurance rests on a tacit inference: more models mean more perspectives, and more perspectives mean
independent corroboration. We show that the middle link can break. When several models share one incomplete
prompt, they can share one reading of it, so \emph{model diversity is not necessarily evidence diversity} and
the agreement can fail \emph{silently}.
In a pre-registered study on a reconstructed, executable-gold benchmark of \emph{underspecified} tasks, we
vary a single factor: whether the decisive condition is present in the prompt---as a task-aware user would
write it---or silently absent, the everyday case in which a user under-specifies a task without realizing a
decisive detail is missing. We ask not whether the prompt is \emph{ambiguous} but whether independent agents
converge on the \emph{same wrong} reading of it, measuring the \emph{correlation} of errors, not just
accuracy. When the condition is
absent, independent agents across capability tiers and three model families (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google)
converge on the \emph{same wrong} interpretation (convergent-delusion rate $\approx\!0.53$ on our
constructed, default-screened items), almost never
signaling anything is missing (abstention $\approx\!0.03\%$); a nominal $k$-agent ensemble then supplies
close to one \emph{effective} independent judgment ($\neff\!=\!1.10$). When it is present, no tier converges
on a shared wrong reading ($=\!0.00$), not even weaker, non-frontier models, and a benchmark built by a
\emph{different} model family argues against a shared-prior-with-the-constructor artifact. We name this
failure \emph{fake redundancy}: under a shared information boundary, nominally diverse agents form an
\emph{interpretive monoculture}. Building on this diagnosis, we contribute a black-box, cross-vendor detector
that flags, at inference time and without ground truth, the latent-premise ambiguity behind the failure. The signal is \emph{latent-premise sensitivity} (whether an answer depends on an unstated premise), not
same-prompt variation: state-of-the-art semantic entropy is near-blind here by construction (pooled AUROC
$0.581$), because the failure is confidently \emph{stable}. Surfacing the premises moves onto the right axis:
a requirements-probing variant already reaches $0.810$, and our detector, which counterfactually pins each
self-surfaced premise and clusters by executable equivalence, tops the axis at $0.895$ (95\% CI
$[0.815,0.966]$). It over-clarifies unambiguous prompts at a
model-dependent rate (pooled $0.402$). Used as a clarification gate with a simulated-user oracle
that supplies the missing convention, it cuts convergent delusion from \cd{} $0.626$ to $0.010$. This upper
bound holds when the missing convention is recoverable by someone in the loop: the detector localizes the
premise only weakly ($0.36$) and cannot supply the fix itself. Because people read agreement as confidence
and rarely register whether their sources are correlated, systems aggregating AI judgments should surface
\emph{evidential dependence}
(whether answers rest on the same, possibly incomplete, input) rather than an agent count.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Unsure about a decision, you consult not one AI but several---GPT, Claude, Gemini, and more---and they all
agree. Nothing on the screen says so outright, but the answers stacked side by side make the point for
you---``$4/4$ models agree''---and it is hard not to read that apparent unanimity
as reassurance; surely four \emph{independent} systems would not all make the \emph{same} mistake.
\emph{Same-prompt multi-model interfaces} turn this into an everyday interaction: the user types one prompt,
the tool broadcasts it to several language models (GPT, Claude, Gemini, and others) and returns the answers
side by side, a computed consensus verdict, or a single synthesized
reply~\cite{chathub2026,openwebui2026multimodel,promptquorum2026home,multiplechat2026,poe2024multibot,googleaistudio2024compare}.
Many present this plurality as an epistemic feature rather than a convenience---a way to ``gain confidence,''
reduce hallucination, or ``let them vote''---and collaborative and decision-support systems increasingly
aggregate AI judgments the same way. This paper is about when that reassurance betrays the user: when the
models agree, confidently and in unison, on the \emph{same wrong} answer, because they never held independent
views to begin with.

The appeal rests on an inference that is rarely stated but easy to reconstruct: because several
\emph{different} models concur, their agreement must reflect independent perspectives, and independent
perspectives must make the verdict more trustworthy. That warrant borrows from an old and
specific idea. From the Condorcet Jury Theorem to the wisdom of crowds, aggregation improves reliability
only when the judgments being combined are competent and carry \emph{sufficiently independent}
errors~\cite{condorcet,ladha1992,surowiecki2004}. Cognitive diversity, not sheer group size, is
what lets a crowd outperform its ablest member~\cite{hongpage2004}, and social influence that erodes that
independence produces confident convergence on error rather than accuracy~\cite{lorenz2011}. A same-prompt interface imports this warrant,
but it also imports its precondition, and it is far from obvious that sending one prompt to five models
produces five \emph{independent} interpretations of the task. We show that the middle link of that chain can
break: \emph{different models do not guarantee independent interpretations or evidence}. In short,
\textbf{model diversity is not necessarily evidence diversity}: when evidence
independence is unknown, especially when inputs may lack a decisive disambiguating condition, consensus alone
is not sufficient evidence of correctness. Same-prompt pluralism can multiply answers without multiplying evidence.

We identify when it does, and when it does not, for a class of underspecified tasks. Consider a
function asked for the quarter that contains a given month. Every model may adopt the familiar calendar
convention (January--March is Q1), while a fiscal-year start lost at an upstream handoff makes a different
answer correct. Every agent returns the same quarter, the pipeline ships a mis-bucketed value into a
downstream budget or scheduling decision, and the ``all models agree'' display never flags that a
fiscal-year start was ever in question. We study such \emph{underspecified} inputs: tasks from which a decisive
disambiguating clause has been omitted, so more than one interpretation is consistent with what remains. This
is an everyday condition: context is lost across a handoff between people, tools, and agents, and---especially
when a non-expert specifies a task by intent rather than by contract---a decisive boundary condition can
simply go unstated, with neither the user nor the interface aware that a decision hinges on it. We find a sharp, pre-registered, two-regime phenomenon governed not by
how capable the models are but by \emph{where the missing information lives}. When the decisive condition is
absent from the prompt---the case in which a user under-specifies without realizing it---independent agents converge on the \emph{same wrong} interpretation.
Weak models are not the culprit: frontier
reasoners are trapped about as reliably as small ones. Nor is it any single vendor---models from OpenAI,
Anthropic, and Google fall into the \emph{same} trap, and they do so in such lockstep that a nominal
$k$-agent ensemble supplies the evidence of roughly one independent judgment ($\neff\!=\!1.10$). And the
failure is \emph{silent}: the agents almost never flag that anything is missing. Supply that condition---write
the prompt as a task-aware user would---and the failure vanishes (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}
previews the setup and result). We call the wrong-consensus failure
\emph{convergent delusion} (a descriptive label for a shared, confident, wrong reading, not a psychological
claim), and the structural condition that produces it \emph{fake redundancy}: apparent
redundancy that, under the shared information boundary we test, is not sufficient as independent corroboration
because all agents inherit the same lossy representation of the task. An agent's default is often a \emph{locally reasonable} inference from the
underspecified prompt, not model irrationality; the harm is the silent, correlated convergence of
many agents on one such reading \emph{without} flagging that the task admits others.
Our goal is to characterize and detect this failure mode---and to isolate the mechanism
(\emph{where} the decisive disambiguator lies) that produces it---rather than to estimate its real-world
prevalence: our items are deliberately constructed and screened by an empirical default check
(\S\ref{sec:benchmark}), and we claim no generality beyond the tested items and models.

Reading multi-model agreement is not merely
an accuracy problem: consensus is evidence only when the agents are near-independent ($\neff$ close to the
agent count $k$), and a same-prompt interface gives a user no way to tell whether they are. When nominally diverse agents share an information boundary,
they can form an \emph{interpretive monoculture}~\cite{kleinberg2021monoculture} whose agreement is an
artifact of a shared prior rather than corroborating evidence.

We present two linked studies. \emph{Study~1} documents the failure (\S\ref{sec:results}): where and why
nominally diverse agents converge on the same wrong reading---and why adding agents, mixing vendors,
or letting them debate does not rescue them. That raises a sharper question: if the failure is hard to
prevent, can we at least see it coming? \emph{Study~2} answers it (\S\ref{sec:study2}) with a
black-box, cross-vendor detector that flags the danger at inference time, without ever seeing the correct
answer. Neither study aims to rediscover that frontier-model errors are
correlated~\cite{kim2025correlated} or to decompose model uncertainty in general~\cite{hou2024decomposing}:
Study~1 isolates one boundary---whether the decisive disambiguator lies inside or outside the retained
prompt, which in our data does not depend on model capability---and Study~2 builds a danger-quadrant
detector for the confident-latent-premise blind spot that semantic entropy cannot see.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper makes three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Conceptual and empirical.} We identify the hidden assumption in same-prompt multi-model
interfaces---that agreement across models is independent corroboration---and name \emph{fake redundancy}. A
pre-registered study shows that independently queried agents, spanning capability tiers and three model
families (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google), silently converge on the \emph{same} wrong interpretation when a
decisive disambiguator is omitted from the shared prompt, and stop converging the moment it is restored.
The failure is capability-insensitive and cross-family: neither stronger reasoners nor a mix of vendors
restores interpretive independence---which locates it in the shared \emph{information boundary}, not in any
one model. A landscape audit (\S\ref{sec:interfaces}) finds a growing class of interfaces offering
comparison, consensus, and synthesis affordances while rarely signaling that all models share the same,
possibly incomplete, input.
\item \textbf{Method and measurement.} An \emph{executable-gold} protocol varies whether the disambiguator
crosses the prompt boundary and measures \emph{correlated error} rather than accuracy: correctness is
decided by running code, never by an LLM judge, so ``models agree'' and ``models are right'' stay
definitionally separate (interpretation sets are constructor-authored and audited, not LLM-scored).
Dependence is quantified with classical estimators---pairwise wrong-answer agreement, intraclass
correlation, and effective ensemble size---which show a nominal $k$-agent system supplying $\approx\!1$
\emph{effective} independent judgment.
\item \textbf{A black-box detector and clarification gate.} Study~2 shifts detection to an orthogonal
premise-sensitivity axis and builds a black-box, cross-vendor, inference-only detector (LPP) that separates
ambiguous from unambiguous prompts at pooled AUROC $0.895$---exactly where the same-input semantic-entropy
baseline is blind---by self-surfacing latent premises and counterfactually pinning them in executable tests.
Gated as a clarification step with a simulated-user oracle that supplies the missing convention, the same
mechanism nearly eliminates the convergent delusion (an upper bound; \S\ref{sec:study2}). We report the
ablation honestly---most of the signal comes from surfacing premises at all, with counterfactual pinning a
real but modest increment---and these results motivate a \emph{dependence-aware} interface that surfaces
evidential dependence instead of an agent count (\S\ref{sec:implications}).
\end{itemize}
We report every pre-registered hypothesis, including those not supported; the capability and family nulls
are load-bearing rather than incidental.

\section{Consensus, Grounding, and AI-Mediated Collective Judgment}
\label{sec:related}
We organize related work around the assumptions our study tests, rather than around technologies.

\subsection{Same-prompt multi-model comparison interfaces}
\label{sec:interfaces}
A growing class of multi-model interfaces lets users query multiple AI language models with the same prompt
and read their responses together. We surveyed nine such platforms and tabulate six representative
ones spanning the major categories (browser extension, self-hosted open-source, consumer web app, developer
tool, research platform). These include
ChatHub~\cite{chathub2026}, Open WebUI's multi-model mode~\cite{openwebui2026multimodel},
PromptQuorum~\cite{promptquorum2026home,promptquorum2026blog}, MultipleChat~\cite{multiplechat2026},
Poe's multi-bot chat~\cite{poe2024multibot}, and Google AI Studio's Compare
Mode~\cite{googleaistudio2024compare} (the full nine, including LMArena~\cite{lmarena2026},
TypingMind~\cite{typingmind2026}, and LibreChat, are enumerated in the supplementary audit). Across the
survey we find a recurring interaction pattern: one prompt is broadcast to
several named models, and the answers are presented in parallel, sometimes accompanied by a computed
consensus verdict, an automatic vote, or a synthesized single reply. Several platforms present this
plurality as an epistemic feature, framing multiple perspectives as a route to greater confidence or reduced
hallucination.

\emph{Audit method.} We identified candidate platforms through public web search for same-prompt / multi-model
comparison tools and included those that, as of the 2026-07-22 snapshot, operationally dispatched one prompt to
$\ge 2$ named models based on \emph{publicly documented} materials only (product pages, docs, marketing copy);
no accounts, private betas, or usage telemetry were consulted. Each platform was coded on a fixed set of
output-side and input-side affordance dimensions. Because hosted products change and copy is not version-pinned,
we treat this as a snapshot of documented affordances and do not measure popularity, usage, or real-world harm.

The analysis surfaces a systematic \emph{asymmetry} in what these interfaces support (Table~\ref{tab:audit}).
Output-side plurality (broadcasting, side-by-side comparison, consensus scoring, synthesis) is richly
provided; input-side adequacy is not. Across the platforms audited, none warned that a submitted prompt might
be missing information needed to answer, none asked models to disclose their interpretation of an ambiguous
task before answering, and none framed the shared-input condition as a \emph{limitation} on the independence
of the resulting answers, so that a
shared, underspecified prompt yields dependent, not independent, evidence. We treat this as a landscape
analysis of publicly documented affordances, not market research, and make no claims about platform
popularity, usage, or real-world harm.

This asymmetry motivates our study: when several models receive the same context-deficient prompt, what an
interface presents as independent corroboration may be structurally dependent evidence that the interface
does not disclose.

\subsection{When does aggregation help? Independence and its erosion}
The Condorcet Jury Theorem's optimistic conclusion, that majority accuracy approaches one as a group
grows, depends on competence \emph{and} sufficiently independent votes; correlation among votes changes,
and can invert, the result~\cite{condorcet,ladha1992,dietrich2013}. Ensemble learning reaches the same
conclusion formally: ensemble error is average member error minus a diversity term, and a common-error
floor persists as members correlate~\cite{krogh1994,tumer1996,breiman2001}. For $n$ exchangeable errors
with pairwise correlation $\rho$, the variance of the mean is $\sigma^2(\rho + (1-\rho)/n)$: as
$\rho\!\to\!1$, $n$ nominal contributors provide one effective independent signal. Empirically, social
influence can reduce the diversity that crowds rely on \emph{without} improving accuracy, yielding confident
convergence around error~\cite{lorenz2011}. \emph{Unresolved premise our study tests:} whether independently
\emph{queried} AI agents form independent \emph{interpretations} when all inherit the same underspecified
representation.

\subsection{Grounding and information boundaries in cooperative work}
Grounding is the process by which collaborators establish mutual understanding sufficient for present
purposes~\cite{clark1991grounding}. We treat the retained prompt as the agents' shared information
environment and the deleted disambiguator as a \emph{grounding-resource boundary}: information available
in-prompt enters the interpretive process, information lost at the handoff does not, and downstream
agreement cannot repair an upstream grounding failure. Their \emph{common information environment}, not
mutual grounding between agents, determines whether convergence is warranted. \emph{Unresolved premise:} whether corroboration across agents can substitute for
the grounding lost at a context handoff.

\subsection{Reliance on aggregated AI advice}
Overreliance on automation is a long-standing concern~\cite{parasuraman1997,skitka1999} that persists with
LLM assistance: people frequently accept AI output even when it is wrong, and recent HCI work shows the same
pattern for LLM-based search and advice~\cite{spatharioti2025llmsearch}. Explanations and confidence displays
do not reliably help; they help mainly when they lower the cost of \emph{verifying} the machine, not when
they merely assert confidence~\cite{vasconcelos2023explanations,bansal2021whole}, and calibrating reliance
generally requires deliberate interventions such as cognitive forcing functions or reliance-targeted
designs~\cite{buccinca2021trust,bo2025rely}. Nor does simply adding more opinions
guarantee a better outcome: a meta-analysis of human--AI teams finds that combinations often fail to
outperform the stronger party alone~\cite{vaccaro2024combinations}. People also read \emph{agreement} as a
proxy for confidence while staying largely insensitive to whether their sources are \emph{correlated}: when
advisors are dependent, decision-makers do not discount the redundancy, and it is the level of agreement
rather than the independence behind it that drives their confidence~\cite{budescu2007aggregation}. This
\emph{correlation neglect} is robust: it reappears once the environment is even mildly complex, even for
people able to correct it~\cite{enke2019correlation}, and it is exploitable, since a source issuing many
correlated signals can steer a correlation-neglecting audience at will~\cite{levy2022persuasion}. A
``$5/5$ agents agree'' display is precisely the kind of cue that exploits this: it foregrounds agreement
while hiding the shared input behind it, increasing reliance while adding little independent evidence.
\emph{Unresolved premise:} whether consensus displays convey evidence or only the \emph{appearance} of it.

\subsection{Algorithmic monoculture and shared priors}
When many decision-makers rely on the same model or data, individually accurate systems can reduce
system-level welfare through correlated outcomes~\cite{kleinberg2021monoculture}. This risk is amplified by
the foundation-model paradigm, in which many downstream systems inherit the priors of a few pretrained
bases~\cite{bommasani2021foundation}. Consistent with a shared prior, alignment and instruction tuning
measurably narrow the conceptual and linguistic diversity of model outputs~\cite{murthy2025alignment,
guo2025diversity}, and writing with such models homogenizes what people produce~\cite{padmakumar2024writing}.
We identify a related \emph{within-ensemble} phenomenon: even nominally heterogeneous models can form an
interpretive monoculture when decisive context is uniformly absent, and recent evidence shows LLM errors are
correlated across providers and architectures, especially among stronger models~\cite{kim2025correlated},
with sycophancy and shared preference training as plausible sources~\cite{perez2022,sharma2024}.
This mirrors a classic finding in human groups: the \emph{hidden-profile} paradigm and the
\emph{common-knowledge effect} show that groups over-weight information all members already share and fail
to pool information held by only a few~\cite{stasser1985hidden,gigone1993common}. Fake redundancy is the
limiting case at the \emph{information boundary}: when the decisive fact is absent from \emph{every} agent's
prompt, there is no unshared information left to pool, and agreement measures only the shared, incomplete
representation.

\subsection{LLM ensembles and multi-agent systems}
Self-consistency samples multiple chains and votes~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}; multi-agent debate can
help~\cite{du2023debate,liang2024} but also converge to shared misconceptions~\cite{estornell2024}, conform
to majorities~\cite{choi2025conformity}, and degrade under peer pressure~\cite{wynn2025talk}. Underspecified
prompts are common and destabilizing~\cite{yang2025underspecification}. Our contribution is distinct at the
construct, manipulation, and metric levels, and it is easiest to see against the nearest work, which
establishes related pieces \emph{separately}: Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025correlated} document that LLM errors
are \emph{correlated} across providers as an observed property; Estornell and Liu~\cite{estornell2024} prove
\emph{theoretically} that debate can converge on shared misconceptions; Yang et al.~\cite{yang2025underspecification}
show that underspecification \emph{degrades} accuracy; and work on algorithmic
monoculture~\cite{kleinberg2021monoculture} analyzes \emph{system-level} correlated outcomes across many
decision-makers. We instead isolate a controlled, pre-registered, capability-insensitive, executable-gold
axis---whether the decisive condition is present in or silently absent from the prompt---that switches the failure on and off while holding the base task,
the agents, and the executable verifier fixed, and we frame it as an \emph{interface-centered} question, when
displayed multi-model agreement warrants trust, measured by convergent delusion rather than by general error
correlation or accuracy. The detector we build (Study~2) differs in turn from clarification-based uncertainty
decomposition (ICE~\cite{hou2024decomposing}; \S\ref{sec:study2-concept}) in target, mechanism, and setting.
Our aim is thus not to re-demonstrate that consensus can be wrong, but to connect a controlled manipulation to
the CSCW question of when aggregated multi-model agreement can be trusted.

\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:rqs}
We pre-registered the following (\S\ref{sec:study}; deviations in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).
\textbf{RQ1 (regime):} does convergent delusion depend on whether the decisive condition is silently absent
from the prompt or present in it? We predict high wrong-consensus when it is absent, i.e.\ external
(\textbf{H1\_external}), and near-zero when it is
in-prompt (\textbf{H2\_derivable}). \textbf{RQ2 (redundancy):} does adding agents add independent evidence?
We test aggregation vs.\ a single agent (\textbf{H1a}) and quantify effective ensemble size.
\textbf{RQ3 (capability/family):} is the failure explained by weak models or a single vendor? We compare
capability tiers and model families. \textbf{RQ4 (dose):} does wrong-consensus increase with the number of
omitted clauses $k$ (\textbf{H1b})? We include a fully specified $k{=}0$ control (\textbf{R1a}), an
interpretation-uniform null (\textbf{R1b}), and a cross-family \emph{construction} control (\textbf{R2}).

\section{Executable-Gold Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}
\subsection{Construct and design}
A task is \emph{underspecified} when a decisive clause is removed so that multiple interpretations remain
consistent with the retained input. Each task fixes a target interpretation $I_0$ and enumerated
alternatives (foils) $I_1,\dots$; an answer is labeled deterministically by \emph{which} interpretation it
realizes (via an executable \texttt{check()}) or \iperp{} if it is off-axis or unparseable. Critically,
correctness and the interpretation label are established \emph{independently of model agreement}: no LLM
judges any output. The gold checkers are deterministic and domain-specific: \texttt{code\_spec} executes the
candidate implementation against tests; \texttt{data\_analysis} compares computed results; and
\texttt{policy\_qa} uses structured exact-cent numeric matching on answers of the form
\texttt{\{"amount": float\}}. These are finite behavioral checks, not proofs of correctness for all possible
inputs, and \texttt{policy\_qa} verifies only the final numeric amount rather than the reasoning path.

We reconstruct 54 task variants across three domains (code specification, data analysis, policy
question-answering). Items are \emph{reversed} so the natural default an unaware solver would produce is a
\emph{foil}, and each foil differs from $I_0$ on exactly one convention axis (e.g., calendar vs.\ fiscal
quarter; floor vs.\ truncating integer division). The set contains 40 \textbf{H1\_external} items (the
disambiguator requires outside knowledge) and 14 \textbf{H2\_derivable} items (the disambiguator is present
in the retained prompt), each at ambiguity level $k\in\{0,1,2,3\}$, where $k{=}0$ is fully specified.
Table~\ref{tab:examples} illustrates the two regimes: in both, an unaware solver produces a natural default;
they differ in whether the retained prompt \emph{contains} what is needed to override it. The 40
\textbf{H1\_external} and 14 \textbf{H2\_derivable} items are \emph{different item families}, not paired
minimal variants of identical tasks, so the H1-versus-H2 contrast is a broad regime comparison. The sharpest
\emph{causal} evidence that the disambiguator's \emph{presence} drives the failure comes instead from the
\emph{within-item} $k{=}0$-vs-$k{\ge}1$ control (R1a, \S\ref{sec:results}), which compares matched variants of
the \emph{same base task} that differ in whether the disambiguating clause(s) are retained---one deleted
clause at $k{=}1$ and additional deleted clauses as $k$ grows, which also opens further interpretations
(combinatorial foils; Appendix~\ref{app:deviations})---holding the base task and domain fixed. Constructing fully matched
paired H1/H2 variants (the same item shipped in both regimes) is future work.

\subsection{Construct validity}
An external disambiguator must be realistic omitted context, not adversarial trivia, and $I_0$ must be
uniquely established by an out-of-prompt convention. We validated each item with an empirical default check
(capable models, unaware, produce the foil) and, for the regime tag, that in-prompt disambiguators are
resolved. Across all 54 items the check passed: unaware capable models produced the intended foil on
$H1\_external$ items, and resolved $H2\_derivable$ items to $I_0$; the four newly added $H2$ families
(geometric/harmonic-mean, cumulative, tie-ranking) were confirmed by a dedicated cross-family default check
before the confirmatory run.

\emph{Gold-construction safeguards.} Because the main item semantics were machine-constructed by a frontier
code model from a vendor outside the tested pool, we guard against the possibility that the gold itself is a confident AI error. The
gold is executable, deterministic, and LLM-free: all 54 frozen pre-registered items are certified 100\% mutually
distinguishable, as are the R2 subset (12) and the two secondary-robustness sidecars (64), for 130/130 total with 163 passing tests. The
adversarial-foil validator fails closed: each interpretation's reference candidate must pass only its own
checker, adversarial foils must match at most one checker, certification requires a non-empty foil set, and any
candidate matching more than one checker raises \texttt{AmbiguousLabelError} rather than being silently labeled.
Every ambiguous item (71/71 across $k{=}1,2,3$) has a matched fully specified $k{=}0$ control; empirically those
$k{=}0$ controls yield $\cd\approx0$, validating that the $I_0$ checker recognizes correct implementations.
This does not prove all-input correctness, but it makes labeling deterministic rather than an AI opinion. The
constructor is outside the tested OpenAI/Anthropic/Google pool, tested-model convergence across all three vendor
families is cross-family on the same foil $I_1$, and R2 replicates the effect under an independent Anthropic constructor tested only on
non-Anthropic models.

\emph{Default-check disclosure and construct-validity caveat.} Item selection is \emph{partly conditioned on
the default behavior} we then measure: inclusion required that unaware capable models produce the intended
foil. This is a construct-validity limitation of the sampling frame, and the effect is nevertheless neither a
difficulty artifact nor a screening artifact (threats and controls in Table~\ref{tab:threats}). Within the
\emph{same} base H1 task, retaining the disambiguating clause ($k{=}0$, fully specified) yields convergent
delusion $\cd\!\approx\!0$ while deleting it ($k{\ge}1$) drives $\cd$ high (R1a, \S\ref{sec:results},
F1/F4), and on \emph{different} families, $H2\_derivable$ items, where the
ambiguity is present but the deciding fact is retained in the prompt so a competent solver can \emph{derive}
$I_0$, yield $\cd\!\approx\!0$ even at $k{\ge}1$: item difficulty cannot explain $\cd\!\approx\!0$ when the
same task is fully specified, so the effect tracks the ambiguity \emph{structure} (the disambiguator's
location). Screening \emph{specifically} for
ambiguous items that elicit the foil could still add inflation to the observed \emph{rate}, and a
pre-registered secondary robustness extension bounds that directly with a fully unfiltered sample
(no default screen): across 24 unfiltered $k{=}1$ items, pooled unconditioned convergent delusion is
$\cd=0.685$ with 95\% CI $[0.521,0.839]$, and 79.2\% of unfiltered items still exhibit convergent delusion.
The point-estimate delta relative to the screened confirmatory rate is $+0.155$ and the screened rate
(0.53) lies within the unfiltered CI, so the result does \emph{not} attenuate, rather than being
significantly higher, supporting H-A14 (no attenuation). Provenance
separation (the constructor family is outside the tested pool, so the screen is not run by an evaluated
agent) and the R2 cross-family construction control (\S\ref{sec:results}), where a second constructor
family builds the traps, close the same gap from a second direction.

\subsection{Conditions and estimands}
We keep the frozen internal condition names and add product-facing labels (in brackets) that map each
condition to a real same-prompt interface behavior. Primary aggregation conditions: single-agent baseline
[\emph{single-model assistance}: one model answers once]; self-consistency ($k{=}5$) [\emph{repeated
sampling}: one model resampled, majority]; homogeneous majority ($N{=}5$) [\emph{same-vendor
ensemble}: $\rho\!\to\!1$ baseline]; and heterogeneous cross-family \emph{aggregation} [\emph{cross-vendor
comparison/aggregation}: different vendors, same prompt, aggregated]. The primary grid aggregates
\emph{independent} same-prompt answers with no inter-agent messages; message-passing debate is not part of
the confirmatory conditions. A scheduled secondary sensitivity pass
adds self-consistency $k{=}10$ (\S\ref{sec:results}) and a verifier condition [\emph{verifier-augmented}:
a secondary check pass]. An interpretation-diverse condition [\emph{interpretation-first comparison}: agents
disclose their task interpretation before answering] was run as a \emph{secondary} method but excluded from
the primary grid (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}), because disclosing interpretations risks leaking the axis; we
therefore do not draw primary claims from it. The cross-vendor comparison condition is the strongest real-world platform analogue in our primary design; a pure \emph{synthesis} condition (one extra model merges all answers) and a \emph{role-diversified} workflow are reported separately as post-hoc \textbf{exploratory} conditions (\S\ref{sec:phaseB}). Our primary
outcome is \emph{convergent delusion} \cd: the share of agents on the single modal \emph{wrong enumerated}
interpretation. Formally, for a cell of $N$ agents with interpretation labels $L_1,\dots,L_N$, target $I_0$,
and off-axis label $I_\perp$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cd}
\cd \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\,\max_{\,I_j \,\notin\, \{I_0,\, I_\perp\}}\ \bigl|\{\,i : L_i = I_j\,\}\bigr|,
\end{equation}
so \cd{} is high only when agents \emph{agree} on a specific wrong answer, not merely when they are
individually wrong (\iperp{} stays in the denominator but cannot be the convergent label).
\emph{Worked example (illustrative).} Take $N{=}5$ agents on one item with target $I_0$ and off-axis
$\iperp$. If the labels are $\{I_a, I_a, I_a, I_0, \iperp\}$---three agents on the same wrong reading
$I_a$, one correct, one off-axis---the modal wrong count is $3$, so $\cd = 3/5 = 0.60$, while per-agent
accuracy is only $0.20$; this item is majority-wrong (three share $I_a$) but not unanimous-wrong. A
same-input resampling detector sees five confident, mostly agreeing answers and flags nothing, yet four of
five are wrong and three converge on the \emph{same} premise. Contrast $\{I_0, I_0, I_a, I_b, \iperp\}$:
agents are individually wrong just as often, but the modal wrong count is $1$, so $\cd = 0.20$---low, because
the errors do not \emph{concentrate}. \cd{} thus isolates \emph{correlated} error from mere inaccuracy.
We report two
pre-registered sensitivity variants (\iperp{}-eligible; \iperp{}-dropped). We additionally report dependence
estimators over each cell's labels: pairwise wrong-answer agreement (fraction of wrong-agent pairs on the
\emph{same} wrong label), Fleiss' $\kappa$, the intraclass
correlation $\rho$ of the wrong-answer indicator, and the \emph{effective ensemble size}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:neff}
\neff \;=\; \frac{n}{1+(n-1)\,\bar\rho},
\end{equation}
which quantifies how many \emph{independent} judgments a nominal $n$-agent ensemble supplies ($\neff\!\to\!1$
as $\bar\rho\!\to\!1$), and an independence-calibrated counterfactual $\Delta = \cd_{\mathrm{obs}} -
\mathbb{E}[\cd_{\mathrm{indep}}]$ that resamples each agent independently from its own marginal accuracy. A
rule-based (non-LLM) detector flags whether an agent abstained or requested clarification. Each cell is
replicated over three collision-free seeds.

\emph{Dependence-estimator specification.} The intraclass correlation $\bar\rho$ is estimated from a one-way
random-effects (ANOVA) model on the binary \emph{wrong-answer} indicator, with the item-cell (a
regime$\times$class$\times$item grouping) as the random grouping factor; the observation unit is a single
agent execution within that cell. $\bar\rho$ is the between-cell variance component divided by the total
(between $+$ within) variance, and the effective ensemble size $\neff=n/(1+(n-1)\bar\rho)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:neff})
follows under the exchangeability assumption that within-cell agent errors share a common pairwise
correlation. Heterogeneous-agent exchangeability is an idealization (distinct families need not
be exactly exchangeable), so we read $\neff$ alongside the model-free pairwise wrong-answer agreement and
Fleiss' $\kappa$ rather than in isolation; the analysis code is released (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). The
independence-calibrated counterfactual resamples each agent independently from its own \emph{item-marginal}
accuracy, so shared item \emph{difficulty} is an alternative account of the observed excess to shared
\emph{interpretation}. The convergence claim rests on the joint evidence from ICC, pairwise agreement, and
$\neff$ together with the within-item $k{=}0$ control, which holds the base task fixed and varies only the
disambiguator (Table~\ref{tab:threats}).

\subsection{Analysis and open science}
Hypotheses, the primary metric, the regime criterion, decision rules, and robustness nulls were
git-committed and frozen before the confirmatory run; every subsequent change is a timestamped, signed
amendment reported in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}. Provenance was separated by construction: analysis
artifacts were implemented and \emph{independently, adversarially audited by a different model family}
(report-only), all labels derive from deterministic executable gold,
and the A13/A14 completeness gate fails closed by reporting incomplete data as incomplete, never as a result.
The pre-registration and its signed amendments are
enumerated in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}, and the benchmark, evaluators, and analysis code will be
released (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
The confirmatory run comprised 5{,}832 aggregation jobs (16{,}461 agent executions) over 54 items, three
seeds, and the primary conditions. We report answer-first findings.

\subsection{F1: Location, not difficulty}
The cleanest result comes from matched variants of the \emph{same} task. With every disambiguating clause
retained ($k{=}0$), convergent delusion is $0.00$; delete one or more of those clauses ($k{\ge}1$) and it
jumps to $0.82$ (difference 95\% CI $[0.73,0.90]$)---same pipeline, labeler, and metric, so the switch is the
missing clause, not item difficulty (\textbf{R1a}, ruling out a labeler or foil-count artifact). The broad
regime comparison shows the same boundary: on \textbf{H1\_external}, \cd{} $=0.53$ (0.60 among parseable
answers)---a majority of independent agents land on the \emph{same wrong} interpretation---whereas on
\textbf{H2\_derivable}, \cd{} $=0.00$. We read $\cd=0$ narrowly, as no convergence on a shared wrong foil
rather than ``fully resolved'': H2 responses can still be off-axis or unparseable (the \iperp{} rate reaches
$15.2\%$ in some H2 cells; Table~\ref{tab:regime}), so the claim is about the \emph{absence of correlated
wrong consensus}, not perfect accuracy. The regime gap $\Delta\cd=0.53$ is consistent across all three metric
variants (Table~\ref{tab:regime}). The effect is a \emph{threshold} at $k{\ge}1$---\cd{} jumps from $0$ the
moment any decisive clause is deleted (Figure~\ref{fig:phase})---rather than a monotone dose response:
beyond $k{=}1$, \cd{} in fact declines as the growing foil space and rising off-axis (\iperp{}) rate dilute
the modal-wrong share.

\subsection{F2: Diversity without independence}
\label{sec:f2}
The H1\_external failure shows \emph{no detectable difference} across model classes (Table~\ref{tab:regime}): frontier
reasoners are trapped about as reliably as weaker, non-frontier models, and a heterogeneous cross-family pool
(\emph{cross-vendor comparison/aggregation}) converges about as strongly as a single model sampled repeatedly
(\emph{repeated sampling}) (heterogeneous vs.\ homogeneous $\Delta\cd=-0.003$, 95\% CI
$[-0.078,+0.058]$; not the ordering we predicted). Because a non-significant difference is not by itself
evidence of equivalence, we ran a \emph{post-hoc secondary} two-one-sided-test (TOST) analysis at two
pre-stated margins---the pre-registered minimum detectable effect $\Delta\cd=0.15$ and a stricter
$0.10$---on the method-matched paired item-level estimate ($\Delta\cd=-0.020$, 95\% CI $[-0.067,0.027]$)
rather than the frozen
pooled row-35 reference ($\Delta\cd=-0.003$, which the TOST does not test). Cross-family and homogeneous convergence are
statistically equivalent at both $\pm0.15$ ($p<0.001$) and $\pm0.10$ ($p=0.0007$), and the reasoning and
weak tiers are equivalent at both margins ($p=0.006$); the weak-versus-heterogeneous contrast is equivalent
at $\pm0.15$ ($p=0.014$) but \emph{not} at the stricter $\pm0.10$ ($p=0.145$), which we flag rather than
overclaim. Vendor and capability diversity did not translate into
\emph{viewpoint} diversity: the shared prior spans families. The H2 reasoner$\times$capability
\emph{interaction} rests on only 14 items and is power-limited (\S\ref{sec:robust}); the well-powered regime
\emph{main} effect carries the claim.

\subsection{F3: Five agents, one effective judgment}
Five nominally independent agents supply the evidence of about one. On H1\_external, pairwise wrong-answer
agreement is $0.98$, Fleiss' $\kappa=0.97$, and the intraclass correlation of the wrong-answer indicator is
$0.89$, giving an effective ensemble size of $\neff=1.10$
(Table~\ref{tab:dependence}, Figure~\ref{fig:fakeredundancy}). An
independence-calibrated counterfactual yields observed$-$independent $\cd=+0.245$ (95\% CI $[0.234,0.257]$):
observed convergence significantly exceeds what genuinely independent agents at the same accuracy would
produce. Consistently, the pre-registered aggregation-vs-single contrast (\textbf{H1a}) is inconclusive
(agreement neither amplifies nor removes \cd{}), as expected when the ensemble has already collapsed at
$k{=}5$. This collapse is
robust to ensemble size: a scheduled self-consistency $k{=}10$ sensitivity pass yields $\neff=1.10$ and
ICC$=0.91$ on H1\_external (vs.\ $\neff=1.10$, ICC$=0.89$ at $k{=}5$)---doubling the ensemble does not restore
independence. Indeed, because $\bar\rho\approx0.89$, $\neff$ saturates near $1/\bar\rho\approx1.12$ and is
almost independent of ensemble size: whether we take the 5-agent heterogeneous pool or the pooled mean
nominal ensemble ($\approx\!8.1$ agents, as plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:fakeredundancy}), $\neff\approx1.10$.
This is an analytic ceiling, not an artifact of one ensemble size.

\subsection{F4: Confident, yet silent}
On H1\_external the abstention / clarification-request rate is $\approx0.03\%$ while \cd{} $=0.53$: per the
rule-based (non-LLM) detector, agents almost never issued an explicit clarification request or abstention and
instead committed to an answer. The ``$N/N$ agree'' a user sees is then $N$ agents committing to the
\emph{same} wrong reading of the \emph{same} incomplete prompt, with nothing in the display to distinguish it
from genuine corroboration (Figure~\ref{fig:silent}). We use ``confident'' descriptively for this
commit-without-flagging behavior; the abstention-silence claim rests on a rule-based (non-LLM) detector
whose human validation is future work.
Wrong-consensus does \emph{not} rise monotonically with the number of omitted clauses $k$ (\textbf{H1b} not
supported as specified): a linear fit gives a positive $k$-coefficient ($0.31$, 95\% CI $[0.18,0.45]$), but
that slope is driven by the $k{=}0\!\to\!1$ step, and \emph{within} $k{\ge}1$ \cd{} declines as the growing
foil space and rising off-axis (\iperp{}) rate mechanically dilute the modal-wrong share. The finding is a
threshold in clause \emph{presence}, not a dose response; the constructive boundary condition is that
\emph{presence} of the disambiguator (H2) removes the failure entirely.

\subsection{F5: Not an artifact of the constructor}
Rebuild the traps with a different vendor and the convergence comes back. We reconstructed a held-out subset
of H1 items with a \emph{different} constructor family (Anthropic) and re-ran it: models from the
\emph{other} families (OpenAI, Google) converge on these Anthropic-constructed foils at single-agent \cd{}
$=0.72$ (0.87 on construct-valid items), so the effect does not reflect a prior shared with whoever
\emph{built} the traps (Table~\ref{tab:threats}). The
main benchmark is built by a fourth-vendor model outside the tested pool, whereas this
held-out R2 subset is Anthropic-constructed (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}), so the study spans two independent constructor
families. A
\emph{post-hoc secondary} analysis measures per-item
cross-model label \emph{concentration} on the R2 subset, using single-agent labels only from tested models
whose family differs from the Anthropic constructor, separating genuine cross-\emph{model} convergence from a
within-cell single-agent error rate. On the ambiguous ($k\ge1$) items, the mean per-item
concentration is $0.711$ (item-level bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.400,0.978]$), and on $4/6$ of them at least two
\emph{distinct} non-Anthropic families (OpenAI, Google) land on the \emph{same} specific wrong foil: multiple
independent models across families concentrate on one incorrect reading per item. The all-item aggregate that
also includes the six fully specified $k{=}0$ controls---whose concentration is $0$ \emph{by design}, since
no axis was deleted---is $0.356$ (95\% CI $[0.117,0.622]$); we report it for completeness but read the
$k\ge1$ stratum as the substantive result. The convergence persists under
cross-family construction (\textbf{R2}); the pre-registered R2 aggregation-amplification metric was, like
R1b below, statistically inconclusive on the primary metric, so we rest the confound rebuttal on this direct

\subsection{Robustness and reported nulls}
\label{sec:robust}
Table~\ref{tab:outcomes} summarizes every Study~1 pre-registered outcome. The within-design $k{=}0$ control (R1a) is
strongly supported (above). An interpretation-uniform null (R1b)
is inconclusive on the primary metric (binary items give a high random baseline) but supported among

role-diversified workflow---also left convergent delusion essentially unchanged on the same items

\section{Study 2: A Black-Box Detector for Confident Latent-Premise Ambiguity}
\label{sec:study2}
Study~1 diagnoses the failure: under a shared information boundary, nominally diverse agents silently
converge on the same wrong reading. This poses a concrete design problem for any collaborative or
decision-support system that aggregates AI judgments: if displayed agreement should not be read as
independent corroboration, the system needs a \emph{runtime signal} that its shared input may be missing
decisive context---before it surfaces a consensus. Study~2 tests whether a system can provide that signal:
can it detect, at inference time and without ground truth, that a prompt harbors a decision-relevant latent
premise on which the model is confidently guessing? We contribute a black-box,
cross-vendor detector for the latent-premise ambiguity behind the failure. Its target is a blind spot that the semantic-entropy line
of work documents about \emph{itself}~\cite{farquhar2024semantic}. Semantic entropy listens for
\emph{wavering}: resampling the same prompt catches a model that is \emph{internally} uncertain. Here the
danger is \emph{steadiness}---the model answers the same way every time because it has silently fixed the same
unstated premise, and same-prompt resampling stays blind. Thus the relevant axis is not
fixed-input sampling variability but premise/context sensitivity: fake redundancy can have low output
uncertainty because models stably commit to the same unstated premise. Everything reported here is the
\emph{confirmatory} detection read, pre-registered and frozen before the run. The detector-gated intervention
is reported below; the Phase~2c AU-Probe white-box contrast was pre-registered but is \emph{not run} in this
paper and remains an optional secondary future-work contrast.

\subsection{Concept: a seed/context uncertainty decomposition and the danger quadrant}
\label{sec:study2-concept}
We separate two sources of answer variability. \Hseed{}---semantic entropy (in bits) over $k$ resamples of
the \emph{same} prompt---is the state-of-the-art hallucination signal~\cite{farquhar2024semantic}: it is
high when the model is internally unsure and low when the model answers the same way every time it is asked.
\Hctx{} measures something different: the dispersion of the model's answer when a \emph{self-surfaced} but
unstated premise is \emph{counterfactually pinned} to different plausible values. The two can diverge. A
prompt can have low \Hseed{} (the model is confidently self-consistent) yet high \Hctx{} (its answer flips
once a latent premise is pinned differently). That combination---\emph{low \Hseed{} $\wedge$ high
\Hctx{}}---is the \textbf{danger quadrant}: confident latent-premise ambiguity. It is precisely the
fake-redundancy condition of Study~1 (many agents share one lossy reading and agree with confidence) and
precisely where same-prompt semantic entropy, looking only at resampling variance, is blind.

\paragraph{Positioning against input-clarification ensembling.} The closest prior method is
\emph{Input Clarification Ensembling} (ICE)~\cite{hou2024decomposing}, which separates \emph{aleatoric} from
\emph{epistemic} uncertainty by generating input clarifications and ensembling the answers against standard
dataset gold---the same broad distinction as our \Hseed{}/\Hctx{} decomposition. LPP differs in target,
mechanism, and setting rather than in being ``better'' at the same task: it targets the
confident-latent-premise \emph{danger quadrant} that same-prompt entropy misses, scores it by
\emph{counterfactually pinning} self-surfaced premises and clustering by \emph{executable} equivalence, and
runs black-box and inference-only without logit access---deployable on the multi-vendor same-prompt
interfaces of Study~1, unlike white-box probes that need model internals. It exposes a blind spot rather than
dominating every baseline; the detection numbers and the honest requirements-probing comparison are in
\S\ref{sec:study2-results}.

\subsection{The latent-premise-pinning (LPP) detector}
\label{sec:study2-method}
The detector is black-box and inference-only: it needs only sampled completions, no logits, gradients, or
vendor-specific access, so it runs identically across OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google models. It proceeds in
three steps. \emph{(1) Self-surface.} A \emph{generic} prompt asks the model to name the decision-relevant
unstated assumptions a competent solver would have to fix before answering. The prompt never mentions the
target interpretation, the deleted convention, or the enumerated foils: this anti-leakage discipline keeps
the surfaced premises the model's own, not a paraphrase of the gold key. \emph{(2) Counterfactually pin and
cluster.} Each self-surfaced dimension is pinned to the model's \emph{own} candidate values, and the
resulting pinned answers are clustered by \emph{mutual executable equivalence} using the frozen benchmark
harness's pairwise equality check (\texttt{\_runner\_compare}; \texttt{FLOAT\_TOL}$=$\texttt{1e-9},
booleans type-distinct, integers exact, union-find components); unparseable or unrunnable answers are
excluded, and fewer than two parseable answers yields \Hctx{}$=0$. \Hctx{} is the entropy over the resulting
answer-equivalence clusters. \emph{(3) Flag.} An item is flagged when
$\Hctx{} > \tau \wedge \Hseed{} \le \tau_s$, at the frozen operating point $\tau=0$, $\tau_s=0.5$ bits
(the low-\Hseed{} ``convergent'' cutoff that defines the danger quadrant).

\subsection{Executable gold-ambiguity and non-circularity}
\label{sec:study2-gold}
The detector is scored against an \emph{executable, detector-independent} gold-ambiguity label. An item
is $\mathrm{AMB}{+}$ iff at least two of its \emph{own} enumerated interpretations produce different
executable results on its test inputs, and $\mathrm{AMB}{-}$ when no deleted axis exists (a $k{=}0$ control)
or the interpretations coincide; the label is computed by running code, never by an LLM judge, and
never enters any prompt. The evaluation is therefore \textbf{not circular}: the gold label clusters the
\emph{benchmark's} enumerated interpretations while the detector clusters the \emph{model's} self-generated
pins, two independent pin-sets. Of the 54
pre-registered items, executable gold-ambiguity yields \textbf{33 $\mathrm{AMB}{+}$, 21 $\mathrm{AMB}{-}$,
and 0 non-discriminating}.

The study was pre-registered and frozen before the confirmatory run (hypotheses, metric, the 54-item set,
roster, $\ge 3$ seeds, baselines, and operating point), and the detector, implemented by one model family,
was audited by an independent cross-family model that verified anti-leakage and reproduced the validation
pilot (full protocol in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).

\subsection{Detection results}
\label{sec:study2-results}
AUROC $0.895$ matters because of \emph{where} the baseline fails. Same-prompt resampling---the signal
behind state-of-the-art semantic entropy---stays near chance on exactly the items whose answers hinge on an
unstated premise: the models are confidently self-consistent right where they are most ambiguous, so the
fix is not a better resampling statistic but a different axis. Surfacing the unstated premises and testing
whether the answer depends on them moves onto that axis: a requirements-probing variant already reaches
$0.810$, and \Hctx{}, which adds the executable counterfactual-pinning test, reaches pooled
\textbf{AUROC $0.895$} $[0.815, 0.966]$. Against semantic entropy's \textbf{$0.581$} $[0.484, 0.675]$ this is
a $\Delta\mathrm{AUROC} = 0.314$ (pre-committed $\ge 0.15$), the size of the SOTA blind spot; per model
\Hctx{} lands in $0.856$--$0.887$ while \Hseed{} sits at $0.551$--$0.606$, consistent across all six tested
models, three families (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) at two capability tiers (Table~\ref{tab:lps-auroc}).

At the item level, \textbf{$0.758$ (25 of 33) of $\mathrm{AMB}{+}$
items} fall in the low-\Hseed{} $\wedge$ high-\Hctx{} zone (pre-committed $\ge 0.40$)---confident, yet the
answer depends on a latent axis that same-prompt entropy never flags. At the frozen operating point
($\tau=0$, $\tau_s=0.5$) the pooled detector runs at \textbf{precision $0.926$, recall $0.758$}, with a
$k{=}0$ false-positive rate of $0.095$ (Table~\ref{tab:lps-op}).

\paragraph{Caveats.} \emph{Explicitly surfacing latent premises}, in
a black-box cross-vendor setting, exposes the exact blind spot semantic entropy documents about itself;
counterfactual pinning is a \emph{refinement} on top, not the whole story. Three caveats keep this claim
calibrated. First, the requirements-probing baseline (assumption-surfacing \emph{without} the pinning
test) is already strong (pooled AUROC $0.810$), so the pinning increment, though real, is
\textbf{modest ($+0.085$; 95\% CI $[0.01,0.19]$ by a paired cluster bootstrap over items, excluding zero)}: much of the signal comes from surfacing latent premises at all, and pinning
sharpens the separation. This is an \emph{internal} ablation---surfacing versus surfacing-plus-pinning within our own method, not a
comparison against ICE: it isolates what counterfactual pinning contributes over surfacing assumptions alone,
on the tested items and models. Second, \textbf{localization is weak} (pooled $0.36$): identifying \emph{which}
axis is responsible is a secondary, recall-limited signal. Third, the \textbf{token-logprob
baseline is not applicable} on non-OpenAI models, so we do not report it cross-family. None of these
undercuts the primary, pre-committed detection result; we state them for calibration.

the danger zone across models; its \emph{precision} on unambiguous items depends on the underlying model.

oracle that supplies the deleted axis's gold convention on clarification (a simulated-user resolution;
real-user study is future work), convergent delusion on $\mathrm{AMB}{+}$ items falls from a never-clarify
baseline \cd{} of \textbf{$0.626$ $[0.490, 0.763]$} to \textbf{$0.010$ $[0.000, 0.025]$}, a paired drop of
$0.616$ $[0.480, 0.753]$, with the target interpretation recovered on $94.1\%$ of items (oracle $I_0$-hit
$0.941$ $[0.914, 0.966]$). The drop is CI-separated and robust across models (per-model oracle \cd{} is
$0.000$ for five of six models). This is an oracle upper bound, and its scope is the \emph{recoverable} case:
supplying the missing convention lets the models recover only when someone in the loop still holds it. That
is a real limit. By construction the decisive clause was lost upstream at a handoff, so the user at the
keyboard may not hold it either; where it is truly gone, clarification surfaces the ambiguity without
resolving it. Even when the convention is recoverable, the number stays an upper bound: LPP's own premise
\emph{localization} is weak (above), so the detector does not autonomously supply the fix, and whether real
users close the gap the oracle models is untested.
The single-model pilot on gpt-5.6-sol is cleaner still (H-B1$'$ net $0.667$ with over-clarification $0.000$;
H-B2$'$ baseline \cd{} $0.626\!\to\!0.000$, oracle $I_0$-hit $1.000$), consistent with the capability
dependence above.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{The strongest objection, addressed head-on}
A deflationary reading of our result is that the agents converge not because they share an
\emph{interpretation} but because the items are simply hard, or because H1\_external and H2\_derivable are
different task families rather than matched variants. The strongest causal evidence is the \emph{within-item}
$k{=}0$-vs-$k{\ge}1$ contrast on the same base task ($\Delta\cd=0.82$); the H2\_derivable boundary
($\cd=0.00$), the cross-family R2 reconstruction, and the unfiltered replication address complementary

\subsection{Consensus without epistemic independence}
Our results identify a boundary condition for applying collective-intelligence assumptions to AI ensembles.
Aggregation earns its epistemic warrant from independence; under a shared information boundary, that warrant
is absent even when the aggregated systems are nominally diverse. The failure is not that models are weak
(reasoners fail with no detectable advantage), nor that they are homogeneous (cross-family pools show
equivalent convergence; \S\ref{sec:f2}), but that
they share the \emph{same lossy representation of the task}. Consensus then measures the concentration of a
shared prior, not the corroboration of independent evidence, and it carries least evidential value in the
regime we study: the input's completeness is unknown and the agents are far from independent
($\neff\!\ll\!k$).
Our target is \emph{silent, unflagged} convergence on a wrong reading. When the omitted convention has a
single correct default, agreement on it is correct behavior rather than a failure, and our metric does not
penalize it: convergent delusion counts only convergence on a shared \emph{wrong} reading.

\subsection{Shared-input failure, not groupthink}
The outcome resembles groupthink---unanimous, confident error~\cite{janis1972}---but the mechanism differs.
Classical groupthink arises from cohesion, conformity pressure, and suppressed dissent among interacting
members; our agents do not communicate in the primary conditions, yet still converge. This points to a
distinct pathway, a \emph{shared-input / representational monoculture}, in which unanimity requires no
social influence at all. Naming this precisely matters: interventions for groupthink (devil's advocacy,
structured dissent \emph{among} members) will not address a failure that occurs before any interaction.

\subsection{Information loss as a sociotechnical failure}
Because the decisive information is lost at a handoff (a grounding-resource boundary), downstream
aggregation cannot recover it. The problem is therefore located in the \emph{pipeline}, not the model: it is
a property of how collaborative systems preserve (or discard) context and provenance across people, tools,
and agents. For CSCW, the trust a system earns from aggregation is \emph{contingent} on external
disambiguation, and it lapses when the interface cannot vouch for that condition. The design problem is then
not to recruit more agents but to expose the contingency, which motivates the interface moves below.

\subsection{Who is exposed: unaware under-specification}
The mechanism is not about careless users; it is about a blind spot a user cannot see. As vibe coding lowers
the floor for building software, more of the people specifying a task are non-experts who describe an outcome
by intent rather than by contract, and who may omit a decisive engineering or domain convention without
realizing that a decision hinges on it---precisely the underspecified regime we construct. Such users may be
especially prone to the failure we study, and their natural safeguards can point at the wrong axis: consulting
several models, or even delegating a review agent, multiplies readers of the \emph{same incomplete prompt}
rather than the evidence behind it. Because the shared input is unchanged, our results lead us to expect
little benefit from these moves (F2--F3; the debate and cross-vendor nulls in \S\ref{sec:phaseB}), though we
did not test that exact workflow. The risk may compound in complex settings, where a user is least likely to
know which convention they left unstated. We offer this as motivation for the interface moves in
\S\ref{sec:implications}, not as an estimate of how often it occurs in the wild (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Design Implications for Multi-Model Interfaces}
\label{sec:implications}
The findings point to six concrete interface moves; each is a design \emph{implication} to be evaluated, not
a validated intervention (we ran no UI study).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Show a dependence-aware consensus display, not an agreement count.} Replace ``$4/4$ models
agree'' with a panel that reports whether the answers can be trusted as independent evidence:
\textbf{shared input} = Yes (all models received the same prompt); \textbf{context completeness} = Uncertain
(the prompt may be missing a decisive convention); the \textbf{common interpretation} the models adopted,
alongside at least one \textbf{alternative interpretation} consistent with the input; and a
\textbf{recommended action} = request the missing convention before relying on the answer. Since agents
themselves rarely ask (\S\ref{sec:results}, F4), the interface---or its designers---should build in the
clarification-seeking that the models omit.
\item \emph{Aggregate interpretations before answers, and keep dissent visible.} Because the failure is
interpretive, eliciting each agent's task \emph{interpretation} before soliciting an answer, and maintaining
dissent channels rather than forcing early convergence, may expose latent forks that a final vote hides.
\item \emph{Route on contextual completeness.} Trigger human review when consensus is high \emph{but}
contextual completeness is uncertain---high agreement is least informative exactly when the input is
underspecified.
\item \emph{Diversify evidence and interpretations, not merely model providers.} Viewpoint diversity
plausibly requires diverse \emph{inputs}, retrieval paths, and representations---not merely different vendors
behind the same prompt, because swapping providers leaves the shared blind spot intact. Our exploratory
Phase B conditions (\S\ref{sec:phaseB}) are consistent with this: the two interventions we tested
(cross-vendor synthesis and one generic role-diversified workflow) did not reduce convergent delusion. We
frame this as a \emph{falsifiable conjecture} rather than an established result: any intervention that
leaves the shared input unchanged should fail to reduce \cd{}.
\item \emph{Preserve provenance across handoffs}, so that the context needed to disambiguate is not silently
dropped.
\item \emph{Audit for interpretive monoculture} across a fleet, not only per-model accuracy.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent The first three follow most directly from our findings; the rest draw additionally on prior work on
reliance~\cite{vasconcelos2023explanations}. All await empirical evaluation (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Limitations, Ethics, and Open Science}
\label{sec:limitations}
\paragraph{Limitations.} Our tasks are single-episode and non-interactive; real collaborative pipelines
involve iteration and human participation, which we do not test, so claims about human trust, teams, and
high-stakes domains are \emph{implications} rather than tested deployments. Construct validity rests on the
reversed-benchmark design and the default check; two items had incomplete foil sets and are handled by the
pre-registered drop-\iperp{} sensitivity. Because inclusion was partly conditioned on that default check, the
scope of the estimate is a failure mode rather than its prevalence; the pre-registered
unfiltered-sample replication does not attenuate relative to the screened rate (Construct validity, H-A14),
so screening does not account for the effect. The abstention-silence claim rests on a rule-based (non-LLM)
detector whose human validation is future work, as is generalization beyond the three tested domains and the
roster.
\paragraph{Future work.} Three directions follow from this study. First, a \emph{human-subjects study} (a
within-subjects design of roughly 50 participants) would test
whether cross-model agreement raises adoption, whether that causes over-reliance when models share a wrong
interpretation, and whether a dependence-aware interface increases clarification-seeking; we have a design
sketch but have not run the study, which would require IRB review. Second, a \emph{confirmatory},
pre-registered test of input/evidence-diversifying interventions (retrieval-diverse or context-diverse
conditions) would extend the two \emph{exploratory} computational conditions we ran post hoc: cross-vendor
\emph{synthesis} (one extra model merges all answers) and a generic \emph{role-diversified} evidence workflow
(\S\ref{sec:phaseB}), both of which left convergent delusion unchanged. Third, the proposed
dependence-aware multi-model interface is a concept that remains to be built and evaluated.
\paragraph{Ethics.} The study involves \emph{no human subjects}: all data are model-generated responses to
synthetic benchmark tasks, and the ``simulated users'' are scripted, not people. The benchmark contains no
personal, sensitive, or copyrighted material; each item is a machine-constructed underspecified task with an
executable gold evaluator, so no human annotation labor was required. All model calls were served by an
institution-hosted inference proxy under its provider terms; we therefore do not infer environmental impact from
marginal billing (the run cost \$0 on the proxy, which is not a proxy for resource use). Instead we report the
run scale: the confirmatory run comprised 5{,}832 aggregation jobs (16{,}461 agent executions) over 54
items and three seeds, plus the smaller exploratory Phase~B pass ($\approx$1.5k additional calls). Additional
Study~2 detection and intervention runs and the A13/A14 confirmatory robustness runs were also conducted, all
at \$0 through the same institution-hosted proxy as detailed in the artifact logs; no model
was trained or fine-tuned. Use is governed by the provider terms of the hosted proxy. The one salient
release risk is dual-use: publishing a benchmark of ``traps'' could in principle be used to make models
\emph{appear} robust by overfitting to our items. We mitigate this by releasing the executable construction
procedure (target/foil axes and default-check protocol) rather than a static leaderboard, so the failure mode
can be re-instantiated on fresh items, and by framing the contribution as a diagnostic of aggregation
dependence rather than a model ranking.
\paragraph{Data and code availability.} For anonymous review, we will release an anonymized artifact
containing the benchmark items, executable gold-checkers, the adversarial-foil validator, analysis and run
code, prompts, model/version records, the pre-registration, and the full signed amendment/deviation ledger
(Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}). A permanent archival link will be provided on acceptance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
When a screen says ``$4/4$ models agree,'' the tempting reading is that four independent systems concur. But
collaborative systems that aggregate AI judgments inherit an assumption that is easy to state and easy
to violate: that agreement reflects independent evidence. Under underspecification, that assumption fails
silently: nominally diverse agents form an interpretive monoculture, a $k$-agent ensemble collapses to one
effective voice, and confident consensus becomes an artifact of a shared prior rather than a warrant for a
decision. The fix is not more agents but a different question. That question can be operationalized: our
detector shows that the latent-premise ambiguity behind this collapse can be flagged at inference time, even
when same-prompt uncertainty stays low. The concrete implication for multi-model interface design is narrow:
replace the ``$N/N$ models agree'' badge with a display of \emph{evidential dependence}---whether those $N$
answers rest on the same, possibly incomplete, input---so a user can tell corroboration from a shared blind
spot. This matters most for the users least equipped to notice the gap: as the floor for building software
drops, non-experts may under-specify a task without realizing a decisive detail is missing, and lean on
apparent agreement for a confidence it cannot supply.

\section{Exploratory Diversification Conditions}
\label{sec:phaseB}
Two \emph{post-hoc exploratory} design interventions were added \emph{after} the confirmatory run and
pre-registered as a secondary amendment (ratified 2026-07-22; Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}); they are never
relabeled confirmatory. Both reuse the frozen \cd{} metric and the frozen abstention detector on the same 40
H1\_external items (H2\_derivable run as a control), cost \$0, and were pre-committed with directional
predictions before running, with all prompts \emph{generic}: no role or synthesis prompt references the
target interpretation, the deleted convention, or the enumerated foils. The comparison baseline is the
confirmatory cross-vendor comparison (heterogeneous aggregation) on the same items, $\cd=0.544$.

\emph{Cross-vendor synthesis} (P-B1: one additional model outside the tested pool merges the cross-family
pool's independent answers into a single reply) yields $\cd=0.567$ on H1\_external, $\Delta=+0.023$ relative
to baseline (95\% CI $[-0.033,0.083]$), with abstention $0\%$: synthesis does \emph{not} reduce convergent
delusion---if anything it launders shared-wrong answers into one confident reply---consistent with the
pre-registered prediction P-B1. \emph{Generic role-diversified evidence workflow} (P-B2: five agents assigned
distinct generic epistemic roles: solve, find missing information, propose alternatives, check defaults, and
integrate without treating agreement as correctness) yields $\cd=0.575$, $\Delta=+0.031$ (95\% CI
$[-0.052,0.125]$), with the clarification/abstention rate rising only to $2.5\%$; the pre-registered
prediction that it \emph{would} help (P-B2) is \textbf{not supported}. On the H2\_derivable control both
interventions leave the already-resolved regime intact ($\cd=0.000$).

This pre-registered \emph{null} is informative: under a shared information boundary, neither changing the
aggregation \emph{recipe} (synthesis) nor imposing generic epistemic \emph{roles} over the same shared prompt
reduced convergent delusion in the two interventions we tested. On this evidence the failure appears to be
driven by the information boundary rather than by how the shared answers are aggregated; this motivates, but
does not prove, the \emph{hypothesis} that effective remedies must diversify the \emph{inputs}/evidence rather
than merely the vendors or the roles. We tested only these two interventions and do not claim a solution, or
that all same-input interventions are insufficient.

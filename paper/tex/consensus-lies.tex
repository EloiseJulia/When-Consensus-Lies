\begin{abstract}
A growing class of \emph{same-prompt multi-model interfaces}---ChatHub, Open WebUI, PromptQuorum,
MultipleChat, Poe, and others---lets a user send one prompt to several language models (GPT, Claude, Gemini,
\dots) and read the parallel answers, a consensus verdict, or a synthesized reply. Many market this
plurality as a route to greater confidence~\cite{chathub2026,openwebui2026multimodel,promptquorum2026home,multiplechat2026,poe2024multibot}.
The tacit inference is that model plurality yields perspective plurality, hence independent corroboration,
hence higher trustworthiness. We show this inference can fail silently, because \emph{model diversity is not
necessarily evidence diversity}: when models share one input, they can share one blind spot. In a
pre-registered study on a reconstructed, executable-gold benchmark of \emph{underspecified} tasks, we
manipulate a single factor---whether the decisive disambiguating information lies \emph{outside} or
\emph{inside} the retained prompt---and measure not just accuracy but the \emph{correlation} of errors
across agents. When the disambiguator is outside the prompt, independent agents across capability tiers and
across model families (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) converge on the \emph{same wrong} interpretation
(convergent-delusion rate $\approx\!0.53$), almost never signaling that anything is missing (abstention
$\approx\!0.03\%$); a nominal $k$-agent ensemble supplies the evidence of $\approx\!1$ independent judgment
($\neff\!=\!1.10$). When the same information is inside the prompt, no tier---including weaker,
non-frontier models---converges on a shared wrong interpretation (rate $=\!0.00$). A second benchmark constructed by a \emph{different} model family
rules out a shared-prior-with-the-constructor artifact. We name this failure \emph{fake redundancy}: under a
shared information boundary, nominally diverse agents form an \emph{interpretive monoculture}, and consensus
stops being evidence of correctness. Our aim is not to rediscover that frontier-model errors are correlated,
but to isolate a controlled, capability-invariant phase boundary---the disambiguator's location---that
switches this monoculture on and off on executable-gold tasks. Building on this diagnosis, we contribute a black-box, cross-vendor
detector (Study~2) that flags precisely this condition at inference time---without ground truth---by testing
whether a model's answer is stable when a \emph{self-surfaced} latent premise is counterfactually pinned; on
executable gold-ambiguity it separates ambiguous from unambiguous prompts at pooled AUROC $0.895$, exactly
where the same-prompt semantic-entropy baseline (AUROC $0.581$) is blind. We derive implications for multi-model interfaces: they should surface
\emph{evidential dependence}---whether answers rest on the same, possibly incomplete, input---rather than
displaying agent count.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
A growing class of interfaces invites people to consult many AI models at once. In a
\emph{same-prompt multi-model interface}, the user types one prompt, the interface broadcasts it to several
language models---GPT, Claude, Gemini, and others---and returns the answers side by side, a computed
consensus verdict, or a single synthesized reply~\cite{chathub2026,openwebui2026multimodel,promptquorum2026home,multiplechat2026,poe2024multibot,googleaistudio2024compare}.
Several of these tools present plurality as an epistemic feature, not merely a convenience: querying
several models is marketed as a way to ``gain confidence,'' reduce hallucination, or ``let them vote'' on the
best answer. The result is surfaced to a person much like a corroboration cue---``$4/4$ models agree.''

The appeal rests on an inference chain that is rarely stated but easy to reconstruct:
\emph{model plurality} $\Rightarrow$ \emph{perspective plurality} $\Rightarrow$ \emph{independent
corroboration} $\Rightarrow$ \emph{higher trustworthiness}. The chain borrows the warrant of an old and
specific idea. From the Condorcet Jury Theorem to the wisdom of crowds, aggregation improves reliability
\emph{only when} the judgments being combined are competent and carry \emph{sufficiently independent}
errors~\cite{condorcet,ladha1992,surowiecki2004}; indeed, cognitive diversity---not sheer group size---is
what lets a crowd outperform its ablest member~\cite{hongpage2004}, and social influence that erodes that
independence produces confident convergence on error rather than accuracy~\cite{lorenz2011}. A same-prompt interface imports this warrant,
but it also imports its precondition---and it is far from obvious that sending one prompt to five models
produces five \emph{independent} interpretations of the task. We show that the middle links of the chain can
break: \emph{different models do not guarantee independent interpretations or evidence}. Our headline is
a slogan we thread throughout: \textbf{model diversity is not necessarily evidence diversity}. Same-prompt
pluralism can create answer multiplicity without evidential independence.

We identify \emph{when} it does---and when it does not---for a class of underspecified tasks. Our study concerns \emph{underspecified}
inputs: inputs from which a decisive disambiguating clause has been omitted, so more than one
interpretation is consistent with what remains---an everyday condition when context is lost across a handoff
between people, tools, and agents. We find a sharp, pre-registered, two-regime phenomenon governed not by
how capable the models are but by \emph{where the missing information lives}. When the disambiguator lies
\emph{outside} the retained prompt, independent agents---across capability tiers, and across three model
families---\emph{silently} converge on the \emph{same wrong} interpretation. They do not make independent
errors that average away; they make the \emph{same} error, in unison and with confidence, and a nominal
$k$-agent ensemble collapses to roughly one effective independent judgment. When the same disambiguator is
present \emph{inside} the prompt, no tier converges on a shared wrong interpretation (Figure~\ref{fig:overview} previews the setup and
result). We call the wrong-consensus failure
\emph{convergent delusion}, and the structural condition that produces it \emph{fake redundancy}: apparent
redundancy that supplies no independent evidence because all agents inherit the same lossy representation of
the task. We stress that an agent's default is often a \emph{locally reasonable} inference from the
underspecified prompt, not model irrationality; the harm is the \emph{silent}, correlated convergence of
many agents on one such reading \emph{without} flagging that the task admits others.
We state the corresponding scope up front. Our items are deliberately \emph{constructed}, and their
inclusion was partly conditioned on an empirical default check (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}); this study therefore
does \emph{not} estimate the real-world \emph{prevalence} or base rate of underspecified, non-default
situations. It \emph{characterizes} and \emph{detects} a failure \emph{mode}---and isolates the mechanism
(\emph{where} the decisive disambiguator lies) that produces it---under those conditions, and we make no
claim of generality beyond the tested items and models.

This reframes a question at the heart of human--AI interaction. Reading multi-model agreement is not merely
an accuracy problem; it is a question about whether the interfaces we build preserve the \emph{epistemic
independence} that makes consensus meaningful. When nominally diverse agents share an information boundary,
they can form an \emph{interpretive monoculture}~\cite{kleinberg2021monoculture} whose agreement is an
artifact of a shared prior rather than corroborating evidence---and, because the failure is silent, nothing
in the interface's ``models agree'' display distinguishes it from genuine agreement.

We present two linked studies. \emph{Study~1} documents the failure (\S\ref{sec:results}): where and why
nominally diverse agents silently converge on the same wrong reading. \emph{Study~2} contributes a
constructive counterpart (\S\ref{sec:study2}): a black-box, cross-vendor detector that flags the failure
mode at inference time. Our aim in neither study is to rediscover that frontier-model errors are
correlated~\cite{kim2025correlated} or to decompose model uncertainty in general~\cite{hou2024decomposing};
it is (Study~1) to isolate a controlled, capability-invariant \emph{causal} phase boundary---whether the
decisive disambiguator lies inside or outside the retained prompt---and (Study~2) to build a
danger-quadrant detector that reaches the confident-latent-premise blind spot semantic entropy cannot.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper makes five contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Conceptual and interaction-paradigm.} We identify the hidden assumption in same-prompt
multi-model interfaces---that agreement across models is independent corroboration---and name
\emph{fake redundancy}: under a shared information boundary, model plurality yields answer multiplicity
without evidential independence. A landscape audit (\S\ref{sec:interfaces}) shows that a growing class of
interfaces offers comparison, consensus, and synthesis affordances yet rarely signals that all models
share the same, possibly incomplete, input.
\item \textbf{Empirical.} Pre-registered evidence that independently queried LLM agents, spanning
capability tiers \emph{and} three model families, silently converge on the same incorrect interpretation
when a decisive disambiguator is omitted from the shared prompt---and that this failure vanishes when the
disambiguator is present. Described with product-facing condition names (single-model assistance vs.\
repeated sampling vs.\ cross-vendor comparison/aggregation vs.\ interpretation-first comparison;
\S\ref{sec:study}), the failure is capability-invariant and cross-family: model diversity and capability
do not restore interpretive independence.
\item \textbf{Methodological.} An \emph{executable-gold} protocol that manipulates whether the
disambiguator crosses the prompt boundary and measures \emph{correlated error}---not merely individual
accuracy---so that correctness is established independently of model agreement and no LLM judge is in the
loop. Dependence is operationalized through classical estimators (pairwise wrong-answer agreement,
intraclass correlation, and effective ensemble size), showing when a nominally $k$-agent system supplies
$\approx\!1$ independent judgment.
\item \textbf{Design.} A \emph{dependence-aware multi-model interface} concept plus design implications:
instead of ``$4/4$ models agree,'' surface shared-input, missing-context, shared-interpretation, and
alternative-interpretation signals and a request-clarification action. Grounded in the landscape gap, this
is framed as design implications and a proposed concept; its evaluation is future work.
\item \textbf{A black-box detector (Study~2).} A constructive counterpart to the diagnosis: a cross-vendor,
inference-only detector that \emph{explicitly surfaces} a prompt's latent premises and flags
\emph{confident latent-premise ambiguity}---the exact blind spot of same-prompt resampling. On an executable
gold-ambiguity label it separates ambiguous from unambiguous prompts at pooled AUROC $0.895$ versus $0.581$
for a semantic-entropy baseline. We report this honestly as a refinement, not a panacea: a strong
requirements-probing baseline already reaches AUROC $0.810$, so the counterfactual-pinning increment is real
but modest ($+0.085$), and localization of the responsible premise remains weak.
\end{itemize}
We report our pre-registered hypotheses honestly, including those that were not supported, and treat the
capability/family nulls as theoretically informative: they are what makes the failure a property of the
\emph{information boundary} rather than of any model.

\section{Consensus, Grounding, and AI-Mediated Collective Judgment}
\label{sec:related}
We organize related work around the assumptions our study tests, rather than around technologies.

\subsection{Same-prompt multi-model comparison interfaces}
\label{sec:interfaces}
A growing class of multi-model interfaces lets users query multiple AI language models with the same prompt
and read their responses together. We surveyed nine such platforms and tabulate six representative
ones spanning the major categories (browser extension, self-hosted open-source, consumer web app, developer
tool, research platform)---among them
ChatHub~\cite{chathub2026}, Open WebUI's multi-model mode~\cite{openwebui2026multimodel},
PromptQuorum~\cite{promptquorum2026home,promptquorum2026blog}, MultipleChat~\cite{multiplechat2026},
Poe's multi-bot chat~\cite{poe2024multibot}, and Google AI Studio's Compare
Mode~\cite{googleaistudio2024compare} (the full nine, including LMArena~\cite{lmarena2026},
TypingMind~\cite{typingmind2026}, and LibreChat, are enumerated in the supplementary audit). Across the
survey we find a recurring interaction pattern: one prompt is broadcast to
several named models, and the answers are presented in parallel, sometimes accompanied by a computed
consensus verdict, an automatic vote, or a synthesized single reply. Several platforms present this
plurality as an epistemic feature, framing multiple perspectives as a route to greater confidence or reduced
hallucination.

\emph{Audit method.} We identified candidate platforms through public web search for same-prompt / multi-model
comparison tools and included those that, as of the 2026-07-22 snapshot, operationally dispatched one prompt to
$\ge 2$ named models based on \emph{publicly documented} materials only (product pages, docs, marketing copy);
no accounts, private betas, or usage telemetry were consulted. Each platform was coded on a fixed set of
output-side and input-side affordance dimensions. Because hosted products change and copy is not version-pinned,
we treat this as a snapshot of documented affordances and keep the language conservative; we make no claims
about popularity, usage, or real-world harm.

The analysis surfaces a systematic \emph{asymmetry} in what these interfaces support (Table~\ref{tab:audit}).
Output-side plurality---broadcasting, side-by-side comparison, consensus scoring, synthesis---is richly
provided; input-side adequacy is not. Across the platforms audited, none warned that a submitted prompt might
be missing information needed to answer, none asked models to disclose their interpretation of an ambiguous
task before answering, and none framed the shared-input condition as a \emph{limitation} on the independence
of the resulting answers---so that a
shared, underspecified prompt yields dependent, not independent, evidence. We treat this as a landscape
analysis of publicly documented affordances, not market research, and make no claims about platform
popularity, usage, or real-world harm.

This asymmetry motivates our study: when several models receive the same context-deficient prompt, what an
interface presents as independent corroboration may be structurally dependent evidence that the interface
does not disclose.

\subsection{When does aggregation help? Independence and its erosion}
The Condorcet Jury Theorem's optimistic conclusion---that majority accuracy approaches one as a group
grows---depends on competence \emph{and} sufficiently independent votes; correlation among votes changes,
and can invert, the result~\cite{condorcet,ladha1992,dietrich2013}. Ensemble learning reaches the same
conclusion formally: ensemble error is average member error minus a diversity term, and a common-error
floor persists as members correlate~\cite{krogh1994,tumer1996,breiman2001}. For $n$ exchangeable errors
with pairwise correlation $\rho$, the variance of the mean is $\sigma^2(\rho + (1-\rho)/n)$: as
$\rho\!\to\!1$, $n$ nominal contributors provide one effective independent signal. Empirically, social
influence can reduce the diversity that crowds rely on \emph{without} improving accuracy, yielding confident
convergence around error~\cite{lorenz2011}. \emph{Unresolved premise our study tests:} whether independently
\emph{queried} AI agents form independent \emph{interpretations} when all inherit the same underspecified
representation.

\subsection{Grounding and information boundaries in cooperative work}
Grounding is the process by which collaborators establish mutual understanding sufficient for present
purposes~\cite{clark1991grounding}. We treat the retained prompt as the agents' shared information
environment and the deleted disambiguator as a \emph{grounding-resource boundary}: information available
in-prompt enters the interpretive process, information lost at the handoff does not, and downstream
agreement cannot repair an upstream grounding failure. We do not claim that non-interacting agents establish
common ground with one another; we claim that their \emph{common information environment} determines whether
convergence is warranted. \emph{Unresolved premise:} whether corroboration across agents can substitute for
the grounding lost at a context handoff.

\subsection{Reliance on aggregated AI advice}
Overreliance on automation is a long-standing concern~\cite{parasuraman1997,skitka1999} that persists with
LLM assistance: people frequently accept AI output even when it is wrong, and recent HCI work shows the same
pattern for LLM-based search and advice~\cite{spatharioti2025llmsearch}. Explanations and confidence displays
do not reliably help; they help mainly when they lower the cost of \emph{verifying} the machine, not when
they merely assert confidence~\cite{vasconcelos2023explanations,bansal2021whole}, and calibrating reliance
generally requires deliberate interventions such as cognitive forcing functions or reliance-targeted
designs~\cite{buccinca2021trust,bo2025rely}. Nor does simply adding more opinions
guarantee a better outcome: a meta-analysis of human--AI teams finds that combinations often fail to
outperform the stronger party alone~\cite{vaccaro2024combinations}. A ``$5/5$ agents agree'' display is
precisely the kind of cue that can increase reliance while adding little independent evidence.
\emph{Unresolved premise:} whether consensus displays convey evidence or only the \emph{appearance} of it.

\subsection{Algorithmic monoculture and shared priors}
When many decision-makers rely on the same model or data, individually accurate systems can reduce
system-level welfare through correlated outcomes~\cite{kleinberg2021monoculture}. This risk is amplified by
the foundation-model paradigm, in which many downstream systems inherit the priors of a few pretrained
bases~\cite{bommasani2021foundation}. Consistent with a shared prior, alignment and instruction tuning
measurably narrow the conceptual and linguistic diversity of model outputs~\cite{murthy2025alignment,
guo2025diversity}, and writing with such models homogenizes what people produce~\cite{padmakumar2024writing}.
We identify a related \emph{within-ensemble} phenomenon: even nominally heterogeneous models can form an
interpretive monoculture when decisive context is uniformly absent, and recent evidence shows LLM errors are
correlated across providers and architectures, especially among stronger models~\cite{kim2025correlated},
with sycophancy and shared preference training as plausible sources~\cite{perez2022,sharma2024}.

\subsection{LLM ensembles and multi-agent systems}
Self-consistency samples multiple chains and votes~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}; multi-agent debate can
help~\cite{du2023debate} but also converge to shared misconceptions~\cite{estornell2024,liang2024}, conform
to majorities~\cite{choi2025conformity}, and degrade under peer pressure~\cite{wynn2025talk}. Underspecified
prompts are common and destabilizing~\cite{yang2025underspecification}. Our contribution is distinct from
the two nearest lines at the construct, manipulation, and metric levels: where Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025correlated}
document that LLM errors are \emph{correlated} across providers as an observed property, Estornell and
Liu~\cite{estornell2024} prove \emph{theoretically} that debate can converge on shared misconceptions, and
Yang et al.~\cite{yang2025underspecification} show that underspecification \emph{degrades} performance, we
\emph{isolate a controlled, pre-registered, capability-invariant, executable-gold axis}---the location of the
disambiguator---that switches the failure on and off while holding the base task, the agents, and the
executable verifier fixed, and we frame it as an \emph{interface-centered} question (when does displayed
multi-model agreement warrant trust) with a convergent-delusion metric rather than a general
error-correlation or accuracy claim. Our contribution is
thus not to re-demonstrate that consensus can be wrong, but to
connect a controlled manipulation to the human--AI interaction question of when multi-model agreement can be trusted.

\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:rqs}
We pre-registered the following (\S\ref{sec:study}; deviations in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).
\textbf{RQ1 (regime):} does convergent delusion depend on the \emph{location} of the disambiguator?
We predict high wrong-consensus when it is external (\textbf{H1\_external}) and near-zero when it is
in-prompt (\textbf{H2\_derivable}). \textbf{RQ2 (redundancy):} does adding agents add independent evidence?
We test aggregation vs.\ a single agent (\textbf{H1a}) and quantify effective ensemble size.
\textbf{RQ3 (capability/family):} is the failure explained by weak models or a single vendor? We compare
capability tiers and model families. \textbf{RQ4 (dose):} does wrong-consensus increase with the number of
omitted clauses $k$ (\textbf{H1b})? We include a fully specified $k{=}0$ control (\textbf{R1a}), an
interpretation-uniform null (\textbf{R1b}), and a cross-family \emph{construction} control (\textbf{R2}).

\section{Executable-Gold Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}
\subsection{Construct and design}
A task is \emph{underspecified} when a decisive clause is removed so that multiple interpretations remain
consistent with the retained input. Each task fixes a target interpretation $I_0$ and enumerated
alternatives (foils) $I_1,\dots$; an answer is labeled deterministically by \emph{which} interpretation it
realizes---via an executable \texttt{check()}---or \iperp{} if it is off-axis or unparseable. Critically,
correctness and the interpretation label are established \emph{independently of model agreement}: no LLM
judges any output.

We reconstruct 54 task variants across three domains (code specification, data analysis, policy
question-answering). Items are \emph{reversed} so the natural default an unaware solver would produce is a
\emph{foil}, and each foil differs from $I_0$ on exactly one convention axis (e.g., calendar vs.\ fiscal
quarter; floor vs.\ truncating integer division). The set contains 40 \textbf{H1\_external} items (the
disambiguator requires outside knowledge) and 14 \textbf{H2\_derivable} items (the disambiguator is present
in the retained prompt), each at ambiguity level $k\in\{0,1,2,3\}$, where $k{=}0$ is fully specified.
Table~\ref{tab:examples} illustrates the two regimes: in both, an unaware solver produces a natural default;
they differ in whether the retained prompt \emph{contains} what is needed to override it. We note that the 40
\textbf{H1\_external} and 14 \textbf{H2\_derivable} items are \emph{different item families}, not paired
minimal variants of identical tasks, so the H1-versus-H2 contrast is a broad regime comparison. The sharpest
\emph{causal} evidence that the disambiguator's \emph{presence} drives the failure comes instead from the
\emph{within-item} $k{=}0$-vs-$k{\ge}1$ control (R1a, \S\ref{sec:results}), which compares matched variants of
the \emph{same base task} that differ in whether the disambiguating clause(s) are retained---one deleted
clause at $k{=}1$ and additional deleted clauses as $k$ grows, which also opens further interpretations
(combinatorial foils, Amendment~03)---holding the base task and domain fixed. Constructing fully matched
paired H1/H2 variants (the same item shipped in both regimes) is future work.

\subsection{Construct validity}
An external disambiguator must be realistic omitted context, not adversarial trivia, and $I_0$ must be
uniquely established by an out-of-prompt convention. We validated each item with an empirical default check
(capable models, unaware, produce the foil) and, for the regime tag, that in-prompt disambiguators are
resolved. Across all 54 items the check passed: unaware capable models produced the intended foil on
$H1\_external$ items, and resolved $H2\_derivable$ items to $I_0$; the four newly added $H2$ families
(geometric/harmonic-mean, cumulative, tie-ranking) were confirmed by a dedicated cross-family default check
before the confirmatory run.

\emph{Default-check disclosure and construct-validity caveat.} Because inclusion required that unaware
capable models produce the intended foil, item selection is \emph{partly conditioned on the default
behavior} we then measure---a construct-validity limitation that could inflate observed convergence. The
decisive rebuttal is a \emph{within-item} one: the \emph{same} base tasks, run at $k{=}0$ with the
disambiguator retained (fully specified), yield convergent delusion $\cd\!\approx\!0$, and $H2\_derivable$
items (the ambiguity is present but derivable from the retained prompt) likewise yield $\cd\!\approx\!0$,
whereas deleting the disambiguating clause on those very families drives $\cd$ high (\S\ref{sec:results},
F1/F4). A ``we merely selected hard items'' account cannot produce $\cd\!\approx\!0$ on the $k{=}0$ and
$H2$ variants of the same item families under the same pipeline, labeler, and metric; the effect therefore
tracks the ambiguity \emph{structure} (the disambiguator's location), not item difficulty or selection. Two
further features mitigate the screening concern: provenance separation (the constructor family is outside the
tested pool, so the screen is not run by an evaluated agent) and the R2 cross-family construction control
(\S\ref{sec:results}), where a second constructor family builds the traps. A fully unfiltered-sample
replication (no default screen) is being pursued as a pre-registered robustness extension; we report no
results for it here.

\subsection{Conditions and estimands}
We keep the frozen internal condition names and add product-facing labels (in brackets) that map each
condition to a real same-prompt interface behavior. Primary aggregation conditions: single-agent baseline
[\emph{single-model assistance}: one model answers once]; self-consistency ($k{=}5$) [\emph{repeated
sampling}: one model resampled, majority]; homogeneous majority/debate ($N{=}5$) [\emph{same-vendor
ensemble}: $\rho\!\to\!1$ baseline]; and heterogeneous cross-family debate [\emph{cross-vendor
comparison/aggregation}: different vendors, same prompt, aggregated]. A scheduled secondary sensitivity pass
adds self-consistency $k{=}10$ (\S\ref{sec:results}) and a verifier condition [\emph{verifier-augmented}:
a secondary check pass]. An interpretation-diverse condition [\emph{interpretation-first comparison}: agents
disclose their task interpretation before answering] was run as a \emph{secondary} method but excluded from
the Amendment~08 primary grid (Amendment~11), because disclosing interpretations risks leaking the axis; we
therefore do not draw primary claims from it. The cross-vendor comparison condition is the strongest real-world platform analogue in our primary design; a pure \emph{synthesis} condition (one extra model merges all answers) and a \emph{role-diversified} workflow are reported separately as post-hoc \textbf{exploratory} conditions (Amendment~12; \S\ref{sec:phaseB}). Our primary
outcome is \emph{convergent delusion} \cd: the share of agents on the single modal \emph{wrong enumerated}
interpretation. Formally, for a cell of $N$ agents with interpretation labels $L_1,\dots,L_N$, target $I_0$,
and off-axis label $I_\perp$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cd}
\cd \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\,\max_{\,I_j \,\notin\, \{I_0,\, I_\perp\}}\ \bigl|\{\,i : L_i = I_j\,\}\bigr|,
\end{equation}
so \cd{} is high only when agents \emph{agree} on a specific wrong answer, not merely when they are
individually wrong (\iperp{} stays in the denominator but cannot be the convergent label). We report two
pre-registered sensitivity variants (\iperp{}-eligible; \iperp{}-dropped). We additionally report dependence
estimators over each cell's labels---pairwise wrong-answer agreement, Fleiss' $\kappa$, the intraclass
correlation $\rho$ of the wrong-answer indicator, and the \emph{effective ensemble size}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:neff}
\neff \;=\; \frac{n}{1+(n-1)\,\bar\rho},
\end{equation}
which quantifies how many \emph{independent} judgments a nominal $n$-agent ensemble supplies ($\neff\!\to\!1$
as $\bar\rho\!\to\!1$)---and an independence-calibrated counterfactual $\Delta = \cd_{\mathrm{obs}} -
\mathbb{E}[\cd_{\mathrm{indep}}]$ that resamples each agent independently from its own marginal accuracy. A
rule-based (non-LLM) detector flags whether an agent abstained or requested clarification. Each cell is
replicated over three collision-free seeds.

\emph{Dependence-estimator specification.} The intraclass correlation $\bar\rho$ is estimated from a one-way
random-effects (ANOVA) model on the binary \emph{wrong-answer} indicator, with the item-cell (a
regime$\times$class$\times$item grouping) as the random grouping factor; the observation unit is a single
agent execution within that cell. $\bar\rho$ is the between-cell variance component divided by the total
(between $+$ within) variance, and the effective ensemble size $\neff=n/(1+(n-1)\bar\rho)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:neff})
follows under the exchangeability assumption that within-cell agent errors share a common pairwise
correlation. We flag that heterogeneous-agent exchangeability is an idealization---distinct families need not
be exactly exchangeable---and read $\neff$ alongside the model-free pairwise wrong-answer agreement and
Fleiss' $\kappa$ rather than in isolation; the analysis code is released (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). The
independence-calibrated counterfactual resamples each agent independently from its own \emph{item-marginal}
accuracy, so a legitimate alternative account of the observed excess is shared item \emph{difficulty} rather
than shared \emph{interpretation}; we do not fully separate these, and rest the convergence claim on the
convergent evidence from ICC, pairwise agreement, and $\neff$ together with the within-item $k{=}0$ control.

\subsection{Analysis and open science}
Hypotheses, the primary metric, the regime criterion, decision rules, and robustness nulls were
git-committed and frozen before the confirmatory run; every subsequent change is a timestamped, signed
amendment reported in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}. Provenance was separated by construction: analysis
artifacts were implemented and \emph{independently, adversarially audited by different model families}, and
all labels derive from deterministic executable gold. The pre-registration and its signed amendments are
enumerated in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}, and the benchmark, evaluators, and analysis code will be
released (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{F1: Omitting external context produces accurate-looking but systematically wrong consensus}
On \textbf{H1\_external}, \cd{} $=0.53$ (0.60 among parseable answers): a majority of independent agents land
on the \emph{same wrong} interpretation. On \textbf{H2\_derivable}, \cd{} $=0.00$: with the disambiguator in

convergence on a shared wrong foil---rather than as ``fully resolved'': H2 responses can still be off-axis or
unparseable (the \iperp{} rate reaches $15.2\%$ in some H2 cells; Table~\ref{tab:regime}), so the claim is
about the \emph{absence of correlated wrong consensus}, not perfect accuracy. The regime gap is
$\Delta\cd=0.53$, consistent across all three metric variants (Table~\ref{tab:regime}) and monotone in the

the prompt is complete and high once one or more disambiguating clauses are deleted (\textbf{R1a}), ruling out a labeler or
foil-count artifact.

\subsection{F2: Capability and model-family diversity do not restore interpretive independence}
\label{sec:f2}
The H1\_external failure shows \emph{no detectable difference} across model classes (Table~\ref{tab:regime},
Figure~\ref{fig:capability}): frontier
reasoners are trapped about as reliably as weaker, non-frontier models, and a heterogeneous cross-family pool
(\emph{cross-vendor comparison/aggregation}) converges about as strongly as a single model sampled repeatedly
(\emph{repeated sampling}) (heterogeneous vs.\ homogeneous $\Delta\cd=-0.003$, 95\% CI
$[-0.078,+0.058]$; not the ordering we predicted). Because a non-significant difference is not by itself
evidence of equivalence, we ran a \emph{post-hoc secondary} equivalence analysis: two one-sided tests (TOST)

detectable effect $\Delta\cd=0.15$ and a stricter $0.10$---on the method-matched paired item-level estimate

overclaim. Vendor and capability diversity did not translate into
\emph{viewpoint} diversity: the shared prior spans families. The H2 reasoner$\times$capability
\emph{interaction} rests on only 14 items and is power-limited (\S\ref{sec:robust}); the well-powered regime
\emph{main} effect carries the claim.

\subsection{F5: The convergence is not an artifact of the benchmark's constructor}
A skeptic might argue that convergence reflects a prior shared with whoever \emph{built} the traps. The
main benchmark is Microsoft-constructed (\texttt{mai-code-1-flash}, outside the tested pool), whereas this
held-out R2 subset is Anthropic-constructed (Amendment~10), so the study spans two independent constructor
families. We reconstructed a held-out subset of H1 items with a \emph{different} constructor family
(Anthropic) and re-ran it: models from the \emph{other} families (OpenAI, Google) converge on these
Anthropic-constructed foils at single-agent \cd{} $=0.72$ (0.87 on construct-valid items). To show this is
genuine cross-\emph{model} convergence and not a within-cell single-agent error rate, we ran a
\emph{post-hoc secondary} analysis measuring per-item
cross-model label \emph{concentration} on the R2 subset, using single-agent labels only from tested models
whose family differs from the Anthropic constructor. On the ambiguous ($k\ge1$) items, the mean per-item
concentration is $0.711$ (item-level bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.400,0.978]$), and on $4/6$ of them at least two
\emph{distinct} non-Anthropic families (OpenAI, Google) land on the \emph{same} specific wrong foil: multiple
independent models across families concentrate on one incorrect reading per item. The all-item aggregate that
also includes the six fully specified $k{=}0$ controls---whose concentration is $0$ \emph{by design}, since
no axis was deleted---is $0.356$ (95\% CI $[0.117,0.622]$); we report it for completeness but read the
$k\ge1$ stratum as the substantive result. The convergence persists under
cross-family construction (\textbf{R2}); the pre-registered R2 aggregation-amplification metric was, like
R1b below, statistically inconclusive on the primary metric, so we rest the confound rebuttal on this direct
per-item cross-model concentration and the single-agent persistence, and report the pre-registered metric
honestly.

\subsection{Exploratory: neither synthesis nor generic role diversification breaks the convergence}
\label{sec:phaseB}
The following two conditions are \emph{post-hoc exploratory} design interventions, added \emph{after} the
confirmatory run and registered as Amendment~12 (ratified 2026-07-22); they are never relabeled
confirmatory. Both reuse the frozen \cd{} metric and the frozen abstention detector on the same 40
H1\_external items (H2\_derivable run as a control), cost \$0, and were pre-committed with directional
predictions before running. Crucially, all prompts are \emph{generic}: no role or synthesis prompt references
the target interpretation, the deleted convention, or the enumerated foils, so this is not a
``we gave away the answer'' artifact. The comparison baseline is the confirmatory cross-vendor comparison
(heterogeneous-MAD) on the same items, $\cd=0.544$.

\emph{Cross-vendor synthesis} (P-B1: one additional model outside the tested pool merges the cross-family
pool's independent answers into a single reply) yields $\cd=0.567$ on H1\_external, $\Delta=+0.023$ relative
to baseline (95\% CI $[-0.033,0.083]$), with abstention $0\%$: synthesis does \emph{not} reduce convergent
delusion---if anything it launders shared-wrong answers into one confident reply---consistent with the
pre-registered prediction P-B1. \emph{Generic role-diversified evidence workflow} (P-B2: five agents assigned
distinct generic epistemic roles---solve, find missing information, propose alternatives, check defaults, and
integrate without treating agreement as correctness) yields $\cd=0.575$, $\Delta=+0.031$ (95\% CI
$[-0.052,0.125]$), with the clarification/abstention rate rising only to $2.5\%$; the pre-registered
prediction that it \emph{would} help (P-B2) is \textbf{not supported}. On the H2\_derivable control both
interventions leave the already-resolved regime intact ($\cd=0.000$).

This is a pre-registered honest \emph{null} that \emph{strengthens} the paper's thesis: under a shared
information boundary, neither changing the aggregation \emph{recipe} (synthesis) nor imposing generic
epistemic \emph{roles} over the same shared prompt reduced convergent delusion in the two interventions we
tested. On this evidence the failure appears to be driven by the information boundary rather than by how the
shared answers are aggregated; this motivates---but does not prove---the \emph{hypothesis} that effective
remedies must diversify the \emph{inputs}/evidence rather than merely the vendors or the roles. We tested
only these two interventions, state this modestly, and do not claim to have found a solution or that all
same-input interventions are insufficient.

\section{Study 2: A Black-Box Detector for Confident Latent-Premise Ambiguity}
\label{sec:study2}
Study~1 diagnoses the failure: under a shared information boundary, nominally diverse agents silently
converge on the same wrong reading. Study~2 asks the constructive question an interface designer would
ask next: \emph{can we detect, at inference time and without ground truth, that a prompt harbors a
decision-relevant latent premise on which the model is confidently guessing?} We contribute a black-box,
cross-vendor detector for exactly this condition. Its target is a blind spot that the semantic-entropy line
of work documents about \emph{itself}~\cite{farquhar2024semantic}: resampling the same prompt catches cases
where a model is \emph{internally} uncertain, but it is systematically blind when the model is confidently
self-consistent yet its answer secretly depends on an unstated assumption. Everything reported here is the
\emph{confirmatory} detection read, pre-registered and frozen before the run; the intervention and the
white-box contrast are still running and appear below only as pre-registered \emph{design}, with no numbers.

\subsection{Concept: a seed/context uncertainty decomposition and the danger quadrant}
\label{sec:study2-concept}
We separate two sources of answer variability. \Hseed{}---semantic entropy (in bits) over $k$ resamples of
the \emph{same} prompt---is the state-of-the-art hallucination signal~\cite{farquhar2024semantic}: it is
high when the model is internally unsure and low when the model answers the same way every time it is asked.
\Hctx{} measures something different: the dispersion of the model's answer when a \emph{self-surfaced} but
unstated premise is \emph{counterfactually pinned} to different plausible values. The two can diverge. A
prompt can have low \Hseed{} (the model is confidently self-consistent) yet high \Hctx{} (its answer flips
once a latent premise is pinned differently). That combination---\emph{low \Hseed{} $\wedge$ high
\Hctx{}}---is the \textbf{danger quadrant}: confident latent-premise ambiguity. It is precisely the
fake-redundancy condition of Study~1 (many agents share one lossy reading and agree with confidence) and
precisely where same-prompt semantic entropy, looking only at resampling variance, is blind.

entropy; SOTA baseline) and $\Hctx{}$ (latent-premise pinning), aggregated across
model$\times$seed cells exactly as in the confirmatory analysis

\paragraph{Positioning against input-clarification ensembling.} The closest prior method is
\emph{Input Clarification Ensembling} (ICE)~\cite{hou2024decomposing}, which separates an LLM's
\emph{aleatoric} (input-ambiguity) uncertainty from its \emph{epistemic} (model-ignorance) uncertainty by
generating clarifications of the input and ensembling the resulting answers, evaluated against standard
dataset gold---capturing the same broad aleatoric/epistemic distinction as our \Hseed{}/\Hctx{}
decomposition. Our detector (LPP) differs in target, mechanism, and setting rather than in being ``better''
at the same task. In \emph{target}, LPP operationalizes and flags the specific low-\Hseed{} $\wedge$
high-\Hctx{} \emph{danger quadrant}---the confident-latent-premise regime where same-prompt semantic entropy
is blind by design---as a deployable detector, whereas ICE performs a general aleatoric/epistemic
decomposition that is not aimed at that low-semantic-entropy zone. In \emph{mechanism}, LPP uses
\emph{counterfactual pinning} of \emph{self-surfaced} premises---injecting specific candidate values and
testing whether the \emph{executable} answer moves, clustering by executable equivalence---rather than ICE's
clarification-generation ensembling. In \emph{setting}, LPP is black-box, multi-vendor, and inference-only
\emph{without logit access}, evaluated against our executable gold-ambiguity label, so it is deployable on
the multi-vendor same-prompt interfaces of Study~1---unlike white-box activation probes that require model
internals. The honest headline is that LPP exposes a failure mode semantic entropy is blind to; it does not
dominate every baseline.

\subsection{The latent-premise-pinning (LPP) detector}
\label{sec:study2-method}
The detector is black-box and inference-only: it needs only sampled completions, no logits, gradients, or
vendor-specific access, so it runs identically across OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google models. It proceeds in
three steps. \emph{(1) Self-surface.} A \emph{generic} prompt asks the model to name the decision-relevant
unstated assumptions a competent solver would have to fix before answering. The prompt never mentions the
target interpretation, the deleted convention, or the enumerated foils: this anti-leakage discipline keeps
the surfaced premises the model's own, not a paraphrase of the gold key. \emph{(2) Counterfactually pin and
cluster.} Each self-surfaced dimension is pinned to the model's \emph{own} candidate values, and the
resulting pinned answers are clustered by \emph{mutual executable equivalence} using the frozen benchmark
harness's pairwise equality check (\texttt{\_runner\_compare}; \texttt{FLOAT\_TOL}$=$\texttt{1e-9},
booleans type-distinct, integers exact, union-find components); unparseable or unrunnable answers are
excluded, and fewer than two parseable answers yields \Hctx{}$=0$. \Hctx{} is the entropy over the resulting
answer-equivalence clusters. \emph{(3) Flag.} An item is flagged when
$\Hctx{} > \tau \wedge \Hseed{} \le \tau_s$, at the frozen operating point $\tau=0$, $\tau_s=0.5$ bits
(the low-\Hseed{} ``convergent'' cutoff that defines the danger quadrant).

\subsection{Executable gold-ambiguity and non-circularity}
\label{sec:study2-gold}
The detector is evaluated against an \emph{executable, detector-independent} gold-ambiguity label. An item
is $\mathrm{AMB}{+}$ iff at least two of its \emph{own} enumerated interpretations produce different
executable results on its test inputs, and $\mathrm{AMB}{-}$ when no deleted axis exists (a $k{=}0$ control)
or the interpretations coincide; the gold label is computed by running code, never by an LLM judge, and
never enters any prompt. The evaluation is therefore \textbf{not circular}: the gold label clusters the
\emph{benchmark's} enumerated interpretations, while the detector clusters the \emph{model's} self-generated
pins---two independent pin-sets, so measuring detector-versus-gold agreement is not tautological. Of the 54
pre-registered items, executable gold-ambiguity yields \textbf{33 $\mathrm{AMB}{+}$, 21 $\mathrm{AMB}{-}$,
and 0 non-discriminating}.

This study was pre-registered and frozen before the confirmatory run, locking the hypotheses, the metric
definitions, the 54-item set, the model roster (OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, each at a reasoning and a
weak tier), $\ge 3$ seeds, the baseline set, and the operating point. On provenance: the confirmatory run
was launched under a \emph{conditional pre-authorization} from the owner that delegated the go/no-go
decision, against a pre-committed decision rule, once a validation pilot cleared the strong-signal bar---not
a self-signed approval, and not a claim that the owner ratified each metric line by line. The detector was
implemented by one model family; the detector method and the validation pilot were audited by an independent
cross-family model (anti-leakage and non-circularity verified, and the pilot results reproduced), after which
the confirmatory run followed the frozen pre-registration.

\subsection{Detection results}
\label{sec:study2-results}
Across all six tested models---three families (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) at two capability
tiers---\Hctx{} classifies executable gold-ambiguity far above chance and far above the semantic-entropy
baseline (Table~\ref{tab:lps-auroc}). Per model, \Hctx{} lands in $0.856$--$0.887$ while same-prompt
semantic entropy \Hseed{} sits near chance at $0.551$--$0.606$. Pooled, the detector reaches
\textbf{AUROC $0.895$} $[0.815, 0.966]$ against \textbf{$0.581$} $[0.484, 0.675]$ for semantic entropy, a
\textbf{$\Delta\mathrm{AUROC} = 0.314$} (pre-committed $\ge 0.15$). This is the crux of the SOTA-blind
claim: resampling the same prompt cannot separate ambiguous from unambiguous prompts here, because the models
are confidently self-consistent on exactly the items whose answers hinge on an unstated premise.

The danger quadrant makes the same point at the item level. \textbf{$0.758$ (25 of 33) of $\mathrm{AMB}{+}$
items} fall in the low-\Hseed{} $\wedge$ high-\Hctx{} zone (pre-committed $\ge 0.40$)---confident, yet the

$k{=}0$ false-positive rate of $0.095$ (Table~\ref{tab:lps-op}).

\paragraph{Honestly reported caveats.} The headline is that \emph{explicitly surfacing latent premises}, in
a black-box cross-vendor setting, exposes the exact blind spot semantic entropy documents about itself;
counterfactual pinning is a \emph{refinement} on top, not the whole story. Three caveats keep this claim
calibrated. First, the requirements-probing baseline---assumption-surfacing \emph{without} the pinning
test---is already strong (pooled AUROC $0.810$), so the pinning increment, though real, is
\textbf{modest ($+0.085$)}: much of the signal comes from surfacing latent premises at all, and pinning
sharpens the separation. This is an \emph{internal} LPP ablation---surfacing versus surfacing-plus-pinning,
two points on the same pipeline---not a comparison against ICE: it isolates the contribution of counterfactual
pinning \emph{within} our own method, showing that testing whether the \emph{executable} answer moves adds a
real but modest $+0.085$ over surfacing assumptions alone, on the tested items and models. Second, \textbf{localization is weak} (pooled $0.36$): identifying \emph{which}
axis is responsible is a secondary, recall-limited signal, not a headline. Third, the \textbf{token-logprob
baseline is not applicable} on non-OpenAI models, so we do not report it cross-family. None of these
undercuts the primary, pre-committed detection result; we state them so the contribution is not overclaimed.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Consensus without epistemic independence}
Our results identify a boundary condition for applying collective-intelligence assumptions to AI ensembles.
Aggregation earns its epistemic warrant from independence; under a shared information boundary, that warrant
is absent even when the aggregated systems are nominally diverse. The failure is not that models are weak
(reasoners fail with no detectable advantage), nor that they are homogeneous (cross-family pools show
equivalent convergence; \S\ref{sec:f2}), but that
they share the \emph{same lossy representation of the task}. Consensus then measures the concentration of a
shared prior, not the corroboration of independent evidence.

\subsection{Shared-input failure, not groupthink}
The outcome resembles groupthink---unanimous, confident error~\cite{janis1972}---but the mechanism differs.
Classical groupthink arises from cohesion, conformity pressure, and suppressed dissent among interacting
members; our agents do not communicate in the primary conditions, yet still converge. This points to a
distinct pathway---a \emph{shared-input / representational monoculture}---in which unanimity requires no
social influence at all. Naming this precisely matters: interventions for groupthink (devil's advocacy,
structured dissent \emph{among} members) will not address a failure that occurs before any interaction.

\subsection{Information loss as a sociotechnical failure}
Because the decisive information is lost at a handoff (a grounding-resource boundary), downstream
aggregation cannot recover it. The problem is therefore located in the \emph{pipeline}, not the model: it is
a property of how collaborative systems preserve (or discard) context and provenance across people, tools,
and agents. The silence of the failure compounds this: without an internal warning, more agreement reads as
more confidence.

\section{Design Implications for Multi-Model Interfaces}
\label{sec:implications}
We frame these as design \emph{implications} and \emph{hypotheses}, not validated interventions: we ran no
UI or human-subjects study, so each recommendation is motivated by our findings and prior work and remains to
be evaluated. Most directly motivated by our findings:
(1)~\emph{Show a dependence-aware consensus display, not an agreement count.} The core design move is to
replace ``$4/4$ models agree'' with a panel that reports whether the answers can be trusted as independent
evidence. A dependence-aware display would surface: \textbf{shared input} = Yes (all models received the same
prompt); \textbf{context completeness} = Uncertain (the prompt may be missing a decisive convention); the
\textbf{common interpretation} the models adopted alongside at least one \textbf{alternative interpretation}
consistent with the input; and a \textbf{recommended action} = request the missing convention before relying
on the answer. This turns the interface's headline from agent count into evidential dependence, and is the
concrete form of the dependence-aware multi-model interface concept. (2)~\emph{Aggregate interpretations
before answers.} Because the failure is interpretive, eliciting each agent's task \emph{interpretation}
before soliciting an answer may expose latent forks that a final vote hides. (3)~\emph{Route on contextual
completeness.} Trigger human review when consensus is high \emph{but} contextual completeness is
uncertain---high agreement is least informative exactly when the input is underspecified. Motivated by our
findings together with prior work on reliance~\cite{vasconcelos2023explanations}: (4)~\emph{Diversify
evidence and interpretations, not merely model providers.} Viewpoint diversity plausibly requires diverse
\emph{inputs},
retrieval paths, and representations---not merely different vendors behind the same prompt; this is the
closing design principle, because swapping providers leaves the shared blind spot intact. Our exploratory
Phase B conditions (\S\ref{sec:phaseB}) are consistent with this direction: the two interventions we tested
(cross-vendor synthesis and one generic role-diversified workflow) did not reduce convergent delusion,
suggesting---though not establishing---that interventions which keep the same shared input may be insufficient.
(5)~\emph{Preserve provenance across handoffs}, so that the context needed to disambiguate is not silently
dropped. (6)~\emph{Elicit clarification.} Since agents rarely ask, interfaces---or their designers---must
build the clarification-seeking that the models omit. (7)~\emph{Maintain dissent channels} rather than
forcing early convergence. (8)~\emph{Audit for interpretive monoculture} across a fleet, not only per-model
accuracy. These implications await empirical evaluation, which we leave to future work (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Limitations, Ethics, and Open Science}
\label{sec:limitations}
\paragraph{Limitations.} Our tasks are single-episode and non-interactive; real collaborative pipelines
involve iteration and human participation, which we do not test. Claims about human trust, teams, and
high-stakes domains are \emph{implications}, not tested deployments. Construct validity rests on the
reversed-benchmark design and the default check; two items had incomplete foil sets and are handled by the
pre-registered drop-\iperp{} sensitivity. Because inclusion was partly conditioned on that default check, we
do not estimate the real-world \emph{prevalence} of such situations---we characterize and detect a failure
\emph{mode}; the within-item $k{=}0$/$H2$ controls (\S\ref{sec:results}), on which the same families yield
$\cd\!\approx\!0$, guard the causal claim against a ``hard-item selection'' reading. The abstention detector is rule-based and pending human
validation. Generalization beyond the three domains and the tested roster is future work.
\paragraph{Future work.} Several natural next steps are \emph{not} performed here and are stated as future
work. First, a \emph{human-subjects study} (a within-subjects design of roughly 50 participants) would test
whether cross-model agreement raises adoption, whether that causes over-reliance when models share a wrong
interpretation, and whether a dependence-aware interface increases clarification-seeking; we have a design
sketch but have not run it, and it would require IRB review. Second, we ran two \emph{exploratory}
computational conditions post hoc---cross-vendor \emph{synthesis} (one extra model merges all answers) and a
generic \emph{role-diversified} evidence workflow (\S\ref{sec:phaseB}, Amendment~12); both left convergent
delusion unchanged, but a \emph{confirmatory}, pre-registered test of input/evidence-diversifying
interventions (retrieval-diverse or context-diverse conditions) remains future work. Third, the proposed
dependence-aware multi-model interface is a concept and remains to be \emph{built and evaluated}. We make no
claim that any of these were carried out.
\paragraph{Ethics.} The study involves \emph{no human subjects}: all data are model-generated responses to
synthetic benchmark tasks, and the ``simulated users'' are scripted, not people. The benchmark contains no
personal, sensitive, or copyrighted material; each item is an author-constructed underspecified task with an
executable gold evaluator, so no human annotation labor was required. All model calls were served by a local,
institution-hosted API proxy under its provider terms; we therefore do not infer environmental impact from
marginal billing (the run cost \$0 on the proxy, which is not a proxy for resource use). Instead we report the
run \emph{scale}: the confirmatory run comprised 5{,}832 aggregation jobs (16{,}461 agent executions) over 54
items and three seeds, plus the smaller exploratory Phase~B pass ($\approx$1.5k additional calls); no model
was trained or fine-tuned. Use is governed by the provider terms of the hosted proxy. The one salient
release risk is dual-use: publishing a benchmark of ``traps'' could in principle be used to make models
\emph{appear} robust by overfitting to our items. We mitigate this by releasing the executable construction
procedure (target/foil axes and default-check protocol) rather than a static leaderboard, so the failure mode
can be re-instantiated on fresh items, and by framing the contribution as a diagnostic of aggregation
dependence rather than a model ranking.
\paragraph{Open science.} We pre-registered the study and will release the benchmark, executable evaluators,
prompts, model/version records, analysis code, and the full amendment ledger (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).
A permanent archival link will be provided on acceptance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
When collaborative systems aggregate AI judgments, they inherit an assumption that is easy to state and easy
to violate: that agreement reflects independent evidence. Under underspecification, that assumption fails
silently---nominally diverse agents form an interpretive monoculture, a $k$-agent ensemble collapses to one
effective voice, and confident consensus becomes an artifact of a shared prior rather than a warrant for a
decision. The fix is not more agents but a different question. For multi-model interface design, supporting
trustworthy human--AI judgment means measuring \emph{independence}, preserving \emph{context}, and treating
consensus as a hypothesis to be checked---not as proof.
